How Long Does It Actually Take to Build a Website

A realistic understanding of timeline by project type, plus what actually affects speed.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 10 September 2026·18 min read

Key Takeaways
  • Starting development before content is ready. This creates a false sense of progress followed by a long stall once development catches up to a content gap.
  • Slow or inconsistent feedback from stakeholders. Especially when multiple people are reviewing without a single point of coordination.
  • Adding new features mid-project without adjusting the timeline. Scope changes are fine — but only when both sides agree on what that change does to the schedule.
  • Underestimating third-party dependencies. Payment gateway approvals, domain transfers, and some API integrations have their own timelines outside anyone's direct control.
  • Skipping proper testing to save time. This tends to surface as post-launch firefighting, which usually costs more time overall than doing it properly upfront.

If you've asked a developer or agency this question and gotten a vague answer, it's usually not because they're dodging you — it's because a single honest number doesn't exist. A one-page landing site and a multi-vendor e-commerce platform are both "a website," but they aren't remotely the same project. The short, useful answer is this: a simple business website typically takes a few weeks once everything the developer needs is in hand, a mid-complexity site with more pages and some custom design usually runs into a couple of months, and a full e-commerce store or custom web application can take several months or longer. Those are illustrative ranges, not guarantees — real timelines depend heavily on how ready your content is and how quickly you can review and approve work.

What actually matters more than the number itself is understanding why timelines vary, where projects typically slow down, and what you as the business owner can control. That's what this article walks through — not a single arbitrary figure, but a realistic breakdown by project type, the actual phases involved, and the honest truth about what causes delays (hint: it's rarely the coding).

Rough Timeline Ranges by Project Type

Before going deeper, here's a starting point. Treat every number below as an illustrative range under normal conditions — fast client feedback, content ready in advance, no major scope changes — not a fixed promise.

Project TypeIllustrative Timeline RangeMain Timeline Driver
Landing page (single page)A few days to about a weekCopy and design approval speed
Simple brochure/business website (5-8 pages)2-4 weeksContent readiness, feedback turnaround
Local business website with basic booking/enquiry forms3-5 weeksForm/integration complexity, content
Portfolio or content-heavy website3-6 weeksVolume of content to organize and populate
Corporate website (10-20+ pages, custom design)6-10 weeksDesign complexity, number of approval rounds
E-commerce website6-12+ weeksProduct catalog size, payment/shipping integrations
Custom web application2-6+ monthsFeature complexity, unique logic, testing depth

Notice that every range has a spread, not a single number. A "simple business website" for one client might take two weeks because they had every photo, every line of copy, and every logo file ready on day one and replied to review links within hours. For another client with the exact same scope, it might take six weeks because content trickled in over a month and feedback sat unanswered for days at a time. Same project. Very different calendar time. That gap is the whole point of this article.

Understanding Each Project Type in More Depth

The table above gives you a starting range, but ranges only mean something once you understand what actually drives the width of that range for each project type.

Landing Pages

A single-page site built around one goal — collecting leads for a specific campaign, promoting one product, or supporting a specific ad spend. These move fast because there's no sitemap to plan, no navigation structure to design, and usually just one round of copy and design approval standing between kickoff and launch. The main variable here is how quickly the copy and any campaign-specific offer get finalized.

Simple Business or Local Business Websites

This covers most small businesses — a salon, a clinic, a local service provider, a small retail shop wanting an online presence. Typically five to eight pages: home, about, services, gallery or portfolio, contact, maybe a simple blog. The timeline here is almost entirely a function of content readiness and feedback speed rather than technical complexity, because the underlying build is genuinely straightforward for an experienced developer.

Portfolio and Content-Heavy Websites

Photographers, designers, consultants, and agencies often need a site built around showcasing a large volume of work — project galleries, case studies, testimonials. The technical build itself isn't usually complex, but organizing, selecting, and preparing dozens of images or case study write-ups takes real time, and that work often falls on the client. Projects in this category slow down less because of design or development and more because "which 40 photos do we actually use" turns out to be a surprisingly time-consuming decision.

Corporate and Multi-Department Websites

Larger organizations with multiple service lines, leadership bios, multiple office locations, press sections, and careers pages introduce a different kind of complexity — not technical difficulty, but coordination difficulty. More stakeholders usually means more rounds of internal review before feedback even reaches the developer, and getting sign-off from five departments takes longer than getting it from one business owner. This is often the biggest hidden driver of timeline length for corporate sites specifically.

E-commerce Websites

Once you're selling products directly through the site, timeline complexity increases meaningfully. Beyond design and development, you're now dealing with product catalog setup (which scales with how many products you're selling — ten products is a very different task than three hundred), payment gateway integration and verification, shipping and tax configuration, and typically more extensive testing since money is actually changing hands and errors here are costly. Product photography and product descriptions are frequently the single biggest bottleneck on e-commerce timelines, more than any part of the technical build.

Custom Web Applications

This category — booking systems with complex logic, customer portals, internal dashboards, marketplace-style platforms — behaves differently from a typical business website. Instead of populating a known template with content, you're often defining and building functionality that doesn't exist anywhere else yet. That means more time in the discovery and planning phase to properly define how the system should behave, more development time for custom logic, and meaningfully more testing time to catch edge cases before real users depend on it. Timelines here are also the hardest to estimate precisely upfront, which is why illustrative ranges for this category are wide.

Calendar Time vs Active Development Time

This distinction explains more confusion around website timelines than almost anything else, so it's worth spelling out clearly.

Active development time is the actual number of hours a developer or designer spends working directly on your project — writing code, designing screens, testing functionality. For a mid-complexity business website, this might genuinely add up to somewhere between 40 and 80 hours of focused work.

Calendar or project duration is the total number of days or weeks from kickoff to launch, including everything else: waiting for your feedback, waiting for your content, time the developer spends on other clients' projects in between your review rounds, and any back-and-forth needed to clarify requirements.

Here's the part that surprises most business owners: on many real projects, active development time is a fraction of total calendar time. A project with 60 hours of actual development work can easily take six to eight calendar weeks, not because the coding took that long, but because those 60 hours were spread across weeks of waiting for approvals, content, and clarifications in between. This isn't a sign of inefficiency — it's simply how projects with a human feedback loop naturally unfold, especially when a developer or small agency is juggling more than one client at a time.

Understanding this distinction changes how you should think about "why is this taking so long." The honest diagnostic question usually isn't "how much coding is left," but "what are we waiting on right now, and who does that waiting belong to."

The Actual Phases of a Website Project

To understand where time actually goes, it helps to see the project broken into its real stages rather than treating "building the website" as one lump task.

1. Discovery and Planning

This is where the developer or agency understands your business, your goals for the site, your target audience, and what pages and functionality you actually need. A rushed discovery phase often causes expensive rework later, so a few days to a week here is time well spent, not wasted time.

2. Sitemap and Content Structure

Before any visual design happens, someone needs to map out what pages exist, how they connect, and what content goes where. For a simple site this might take a day. For a large corporate site with multiple departments or service lines, this can take a week or more of back-and-forth.

3. UX/UI Design

This is where the visual direction, layout, and user flow get designed — often starting with a homepage concept for approval before the rest of the pages follow the same visual language. Design revisions are one of the most common places projects slow down, simply because "I'll know it when I see it" feedback takes longer to resolve than specific, structured feedback.

4. Development

The actual coding — turning approved designs into a working website with the functionality you need. Contrary to what many business owners assume, this is often not the phase that eats the most calendar time on a well-scoped project. A competent developer can build a well-defined page in hours, not weeks. Development stalls happen mostly when the scope wasn't locked down before this phase started.

5. Content Integration

Populating the built pages with your actual copy, images, and product or service details. If this content isn't ready when development finishes, the whole project sits waiting — this is one of the single biggest sources of delay across real projects.

6. Integrations and Functionality Testing

Contact forms, payment gateways, booking systems, WhatsApp click-to-chat, analytics, third-party APIs — each integration needs to be connected and tested. More integrations generally mean more testing time, and third-party services occasionally have their own delays (API approvals, verification steps) outside anyone's direct control.

7. Revisions

Almost every project goes through at least one or two rounds of revision after the initial build is reviewed. How long this takes depends entirely on how quickly and how clearly feedback comes back — a single consolidated round of feedback delivered promptly resolves far faster than feedback that arrives in five separate messages over two weeks.

8. SEO and Technical Checks

Page titles, meta descriptions, image alt text, site speed checks, mobile responsiveness testing, and basic technical SEO groundwork. This is usually a background task that runs alongside other phases rather than a separate block of weeks.

9. Deployment and Launch

Moving the finished site to live hosting, connecting the domain, setting up SSL, and doing final pre-launch checks. For most projects this is a matter of hours to a day or two, assuming domain and hosting access are sorted out in advance.

10. Post-Launch Fixes

Small adjustments after real users start interacting with the live site. This isn't really part of the "build timeline" in the traditional sense, but it's worth knowing it exists as its own short tail-end phase.

What Usually Causes Delays (It's Rarely the Coding)

This is probably the most useful section of this entire article, because it directly contradicts what most people assume about why website projects run long. In practice, the technical development work is rarely the bottleneck. Delays generally fall into two broad categories — things that originate on the client side, and things that originate on the development side — and it's worth being honest about both.

Client-Side Delays

Slow feedback cycles. A design or draft goes out for review, and then nothing happens for four or five days. Multiply that by three or four review rounds across a project, and you've added two to three weeks of pure waiting time that has nothing to do with how fast the actual work is happening. This is the single most common — and most avoidable — source of delay.

Content and assets not ready. Development finishes, and the site is ready for real copy, product photos, service descriptions, and logo files — except none of it exists yet, or it's scattered across someone's phone, an old website, and a few WhatsApp chats. Gathering and organizing this content after the fact routinely adds weeks to a project that could have launched on time if this work happened in parallel with design and development.

Unclear requirements at the start. When a project kicks off without a clear answer to "what pages do we actually need" or "what should this booking form actually collect," the developer is often forced to guess, build something reasonable, and then wait for correction — which is slower than getting it right the first time.

Repeated scope changes. Related to but distinct from a single planned addition — this is when new requests keep arriving throughout the project rather than being consolidated into a single, agreed set of changes. Each new addition tends to reopen decisions that were already considered settled.

Missing brand assets. Logo files that only exist as a low-resolution image pulled from an old Facebook post, no brand colors defined anywhere, no existing photography — these gaps often aren't discovered until development is already underway, at which point sourcing or recreating them becomes a mid-project scramble.

Delayed or conflicting feedback from multiple people. When the person reviewing changes from message to message, or three people give three different opinions on the same design, resolving that disagreement takes real calendar time that has nothing to do with build complexity.

Development-Side Delays

It's worth being equally honest that development-side issues do occasionally contribute to delays, even if they're the less common cause. These include:

Unclear scope on the agency's side. If a developer starts building without a properly documented scope, the risk of rework increases, which can add time later.

Poor initial planning. Skipping or rushing the discovery and sitemap phases to "get started faster" often backfires, producing restructuring work partway through that a bit more upfront planning would have avoided.

Technical debt or platform limitations. Occasionally a chosen platform or approach turns out to be a poor fit for a requirement that surfaces mid-project, requiring a change in technical approach.

Third-party dependency delays. Payment gateway account verification, domain transfers, SSL provisioning, or external API approvals sometimes run on their own timelines that neither the client nor the developer directly controls.

Integration complexity underestimated upfront. A "simple" integration with an external booking system or CRM sometimes turns out to have more edge cases than expected once development actually begins.

Inadequate testing before showing work. Sending a draft for review before basic functionality has been checked can trigger avoidable feedback rounds that a bit more internal QA would have caught first.

In a well-run project, client-side factors tend to account for the majority of calendar-time delays, simply because they involve waiting on decisions and materials that only the business owner can provide. That's not a criticism — it's just where the actual bottleneck usually sits, and knowing that helps you plan accordingly.

How to Speed Up Your Own Timeline

Here's the encouraging part: the biggest levers for a faster timeline are things you, as the business owner, actually control.

  • Have your content ready before development starts. Copy, product or service details, logo files, high-quality photos — gather these in parallel with the design phase, not after.
  • Give prompt, consolidated feedback. Instead of sending five separate messages over a week as thoughts occur to you, review the whole draft once and send one complete round of feedback.
  • Lock scope before development begins. Decide what's in the initial launch and what can wait for a version two. Adding features mid-build is the fastest way to extend a timeline unexpectedly.
  • Designate one final decision-maker. When feedback comes from three different people with three different opinions, resolving conflicting input takes real time. One clear approver speeds everything up.
  • Sort out domain and hosting access early. Don't wait until launch week to discover nobody remembers the domain registrar login.

Two Real-World-Style Examples

To make the difference between a fast and a slow timeline concrete, here are two illustrative examples built around a similar project scope — a small retail business wanting a website with a product catalog and photos.

Example one — delayed by missing content. A boutique clothing store in a mid-sized Indian city commissioned a website with a product showcase section. Design and development moved smoothly and were ready for content within three weeks. But professional product photography hadn't been arranged, and once it was, the photographer had a two-week backlog before the shoot could even happen, followed by another week for editing and delivery. The website itself was functionally ready in three weeks; it launched in closer to eight, entirely because of a content dependency that wasn't planned for in parallel with development.

Example two — accelerated by preparation. A different small business, a home services provider, arrived at their first call with all copy already written, existing photos ready to go, and a single owner responsible for all approvals. Every review round received same-day feedback. A comparable-scope project that might normally take five to six weeks launched in just over three, not because the developer worked faster, but because there was essentially no waiting time built into the schedule at any point.

Same category of website, similar technical complexity, dramatically different calendar outcomes — driven almost entirely by content readiness and feedback speed rather than anything to do with the build itself.

Does Paying More Make It Faster?

Sometimes, to a point. A larger team can work on different pieces of a project in parallel — one person on design, another on backend functionality, another on content integration — which can compress calendar time compared to a single developer working through everything sequentially. Rush timelines are also sometimes possible for an additional cost, since it may require reprioritizing other client work.

But budget has real limits here. No amount of extra spending fixes a project where content isn't ready or where feedback sits unanswered for a week at a time — those delays exist entirely on the client side of the relationship. A realistic way to think about it: budget can help compress the development-side timeline somewhat, but it does very little for the waiting-side timeline. Content readiness and feedback speed often matter just as much as, or more than, budget alone.

Template/WordPress vs Custom: Timeline Differences

The platform you choose has a real effect on speed. Template-based platforms like WordPress generally launch faster than a website built fully custom from scratch, because a lot of the underlying structure and common functionality already exists and just needs to be configured and styled rather than built from zero. A custom-coded website, by contrast, often takes longer upfront because more is being built specifically for your business rather than adapted from an existing framework — though it can offer more flexibility long-term. If your comparison of these two approaches interests you further, our article on WordPress vs custom-coded websites goes into that trade-off in more depth.

Neither approach is universally "better" — it depends on your specific functionality needs, budget, and how much long-term flexibility matters to your business.

A Realistic Project Timeline Example

To make this concrete, here's an illustrative week-by-week example for a mid-complexity business website — say, 10-12 pages with a contact form, a blog section, and a service booking enquiry form. This is one example under reasonably good conditions, not a universal formula.

  • Week 1: Discovery call, sitemap drafted and approved, content gathering begins on the client side.
  • Week 2: Homepage design concept created and shared for review.
  • Week 3: Homepage design approved (with one revision round); remaining page designs begin; content continues arriving.
  • Week 4: Development begins on approved pages; design for remaining pages wraps up.
  • Week 5: Development continues; content integration begins on completed pages.
  • Week 6: Forms and integrations connected and tested; full site review draft sent to client.
  • Week 7: Client feedback incorporated; SEO and technical checks run; final pre-launch review.
  • Week 8: Deployment, domain connection, and launch.

Compress the feedback turnaround in weeks 3 and 7 to same-day responses, and this timeline can shrink meaningfully. Stretch it to a week of silence at each review point, and eight weeks can easily become twelve.

What Should Be Ready Before Website Development Starts?

Use this as a practical pre-project checklist. The more of this you have ready before the project kicks off, the faster the whole thing moves.

  • Business name, tagline, and core messaging you want to communicate
  • Written copy for each page (or at least detailed notes if copywriting is part of the service)
  • Logo files in a usable format
  • Product or service photos, or a clear plan for where these will come from
  • A list of every page you actually need, and roughly what goes on each
  • Any specific functionality requirements (booking, payments, forms, integrations)
  • Domain and hosting access, or a plan for purchasing them
  • Agreement internally on who the final decision-maker is for approvals

How to Ask a Developer or Agency for a Realistic Timeline

When you're evaluating a developer or agency, a vague "a few weeks" answer isn't very useful on its own. Instead, ask questions that reveal how they actually plan and communicate:

  • "Can you break the timeline down by phase, not just give me one final date?"
  • "What do you need from me, and by when, to hit that timeline?"
  • "What's your typical feedback turnaround expectation from clients?"
  • "What usually causes your projects to run longer than planned?"
  • "How many revision rounds are included before additional rounds cost extra?"

An agency that can answer these clearly and honestly — including admitting where delays typically come from — is generally more trustworthy than one that just promises a single fixed date without context. Be genuinely cautious of anyone who gives an extremely aggressive timeline without asking you a single question about your content readiness, your approval process, or how many pages and features you actually need — a realistic estimate almost always requires understanding your specific project first, not a generic number quoted before any discovery conversation has happened.

It's also worth asking directly what happens if you, the client, are the reason a review round takes longer than expected. A good agency will be upfront that this shifts the calendar date without necessarily meaning something has gone wrong on their end — and will usually be willing to send you periodic status updates so you always know whether the project is waiting on them or waiting on you.

Planning Around a Specific Launch Date

If your website needs to be live by a specific event — a product launch, a festival sale period, an investor meeting, a marketing campaign start date — build in buffer time rather than treating the illustrative ranges above as a countdown clock ending exactly on your deadline. A reasonable approach is to work backward from your actual deadline, subtract at least one to two weeks of buffer for the inevitable unplanned delay, and use that adjusted date as the real target you communicate to your developer. Flag the hard deadline explicitly and early, since a developer who knows about a fixed date in advance can often prioritize your feedback rounds and content requests differently than if the deadline surfaces only in the final week.

Common Mistakes That Extend Timelines

  • Starting development before content is ready. This creates a false sense of progress followed by a long stall once development catches up to a content gap.
  • Slow or inconsistent feedback from stakeholders. Especially when multiple people are reviewing without a single point of coordination.
  • Adding new features mid-project without adjusting the timeline. Scope changes are fine — but only when both sides agree on what that change does to the schedule.
  • Underestimating third-party dependencies. Payment gateway approvals, domain transfers, and some API integrations have their own timelines outside anyone's direct control.
  • Skipping proper testing to save time. This tends to surface as post-launch firefighting, which usually costs more time overall than doing it properly upfront.

The Bottom Line

There's no single honest answer to "how long does it take to build a website" because the real answer depends on project type, how ready your content is, and how quickly decisions get made on your end. What you can control — having content ready, giving fast and consolidated feedback, locking scope early, and picking one clear decision-maker — usually has a bigger impact on your actual launch date than anything else, including budget. Think in terms of project phases rather than a single number, and ask any developer or agency you're evaluating to walk you through their own version of that breakdown before you commit to a date. Timeline and cost tend to move together, so it is worth reading alongside the breakdown of what actually drives website cost when you are scoping a realistic project.
