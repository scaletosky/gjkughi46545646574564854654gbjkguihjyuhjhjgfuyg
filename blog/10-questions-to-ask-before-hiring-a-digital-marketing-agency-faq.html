10 Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Digital Marketing Agency (FAQ)

A practical due-diligence checklist that builds confidence in their final decision, framed to work for evaluating any agency.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 10 September 2026·19 min read

Key Takeaways

Hiring a digital marketing agency usually comes down to a handful of conversations and a proposal document, and it's genuinely hard to tell from the outside which agency actually has substance behind the pitch. Confident language, a polished deck, and an impressive-looking portfolio are easy to produce regardless of whether an agency will actually deliver — the difference tends to show up in how they answer direct questions, not in how they present themselves.

This isn't a checklist written to make Scale To Sky look good by comparison. It's meant to work the same way with any digital marketing agency you're evaluating, including ones that have nothing to do with us. The right agency for your business isn't necessarily the cheapest, the one with the most followers, or the one with the slickest pitch — it's the one that actually understands your business, communicates clearly, reports honestly, and is transparent about scope, ownership, and what happens if things don't work out. The ten questions below, and how to read the answers, are how you find that out before signing anything.

Why These Questions Matter More Than the Agency's Pitch

A confident sales conversation is not evidence of competence — it's evidence that someone is good at sales conversations, which is a different skill from actually running effective campaigns or growing a business's presence. That's not a cynical statement about the industry; it's simply a reason to treat the pitch itself as a starting point for questions, not as the answer.

The value of asking direct, specific questions isn't really about memorizing the "correct" response to each one — there often isn't a single correct answer, since good agencies differ in their approach. The value is in how someone answers: whether they give a specific, grounded response drawing on real experience, or a vague, generically reassuring one that could apply to any client in any industry. That distinction is far more revealing than anything in a polished proposal document.

Question 1: What Results Have You Achieved for Similar Businesses?

Why this matters. This is usually the first real test of substance versus polish. An agency that has actually delivered results for businesses like yours should be able to talk about them specifically — not necessarily your exact industry, but a comparable business model, sales cycle, or customer type.

What a strong answer looks like. A specific example: the type of business, roughly what the starting situation was, what was actually done, and what changed — described honestly, including any caveats or context that affected the outcome. A strong answer often includes a specific timeframe and is comfortable being asked follow-up questions about it.

What should prompt further investigation. Vague claims like "we've helped tons of businesses grow" with no specifics offered, results described only in growth percentages with no baseline context (a jump from 10 to 20 followers is technically a 100% increase and means almost nothing), or reluctance to name even the type of business when asked directly.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. If comfortable sharing, a case study, screenshot of reporting, or reference contact for a genuinely comparable past or current client.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming an agency without a large portfolio of famous logos is automatically weaker. A smaller agency or an individual specialist with fewer but more relevant, verifiable examples can be a stronger fit than a large agency whose visible case studies are all from a very different kind of business than yours.

Practical example. A local clinic asking an agency about past healthcare or local-service clients might hear one agency describe, specifically, how they handled patient-privacy-conscious ad creative and local geo-targeting for a similar clinic, while another agency simply says "we've worked with lots of clients in different industries" without offering a single relevant detail unprompted — that contrast alone is informative.

Question 2: How Do You Measure and Report Success?

Why this matters. This question separates agencies that think in terms of your actual business goals from ones that think primarily in terms of platform activity metrics that look good in a slide but don't necessarily move your business forward.

What a strong answer looks like. A clear explanation of which metrics they'll track and report on, tied explicitly to your stated goals — leads, enquiries, sales, cost per result — alongside supporting metrics like reach and engagement, with an explanation of how the two connect. A strong answer acknowledges that not everything can be perfectly tracked and is upfront about tracking limitations rather than promising flawless attribution.

What should prompt further investigation. An answer that leans entirely on vanity metrics — followers, likes, impressions — as the primary measure of success, especially for a business whose actual goal is leads or sales rather than brand reach alone.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. A sample report or reporting template, so you can see exactly what a monthly update actually looks like before committing.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming more metrics in a report automatically means better reporting. A report with fifteen dashboard charts and no clear narrative connecting them to your business goals is less useful than a shorter report that clearly explains three or four metrics that actually matter and what they mean for your business this month.

Question 3: Who Will Actually Be Working on My Account?

Why this matters. The person pitching you is often a senior salesperson or account director, and the person actually managing your day-to-day work can be someone else entirely, sometimes junior, sometimes shared across many other accounts. Knowing who's actually doing the work — and how experienced they are — matters more than who's in the sales meeting.

What a strong answer looks like. A clear, specific description of the actual team structure: who manages strategy, who executes content or ads, how many other accounts that person typically handles at once, and who your main point of contact will be for day-to-day communication.

What should prompt further investigation. Vagueness about who specifically will work on your account, or reluctance to confirm whether the senior person in the sales conversation will have any ongoing involvement at all.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. Names and roles of the specific team members assigned to your account, and how many other client accounts they're currently responsible for.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming a bigger agency automatically means more senior attention on your account. In practice, larger agencies sometimes spread account managers across more clients than a smaller agency does, which can mean less individual attention despite the agency's larger overall size.

Question 4: What's Included in the Retainer, and What Costs Extra?

Why this matters. A monthly fee sounds simple until you discover, partway through the relationship, that things you assumed were included — extra revision rounds, ad creative production, a specific number of posts, reporting calls — are actually billed separately. Clarifying scope explicitly, in writing, prevents this kind of friction later.

What a strong answer looks like. A specific, itemized breakdown: exactly how many posts, reels, or ads are included per month, how many revision rounds, whether ad spend is separate from the management fee, and what specifically would trigger an additional cost.

What should prompt further investigation. A single all-in number with no breakdown, or a proposal that describes deliverables using vague terms like "regular content" and "ongoing optimization" without specific quantities or definitions.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. A written scope of work listing exact deliverables per month, separate from the retainer amount itself, ideally as part of the contract rather than a separate informal understanding.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming ad spend is included in the management fee. In most agency arrangements, the fee you pay the agency for managing campaigns is entirely separate from the media budget you spend directly with Meta, Google, or another platform — conflating the two is one of the most common sources of budget confusion for first-time agency clients.

Question 5: What's Your Communication and Reporting Cadence?

Why this matters. Mismatched expectations about how often you'll hear from an agency, and in what format, is one of the most common sources of frustration in an ongoing agency relationship — even when the actual work itself is going reasonably well.

What a strong answer looks like. A specific cadence: for example, a monthly reporting call plus a written report, with a defined response time for questions in between (same business day, within 24 hours, or similar), and a named point of contact rather than a generic support inbox.

What should prompt further investigation. "We'll keep you updated" with no specifics about frequency, format, or who you'd actually be talking to.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. The specific cadence and format written into the contract or scope of work, not left as a verbal assurance from the sales conversation.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming more frequent communication is always better. A daily check-in for a slow-moving, long-term SEO or brand-building campaign can actually be unnecessary noise — what matters is that the cadence matches the pace at which the work itself actually produces meaningful updates.

Question 6: What Happens If Results Don't Meet Expectations?

Why this matters. Not every campaign performs as hoped immediately, and how an agency responds when that happens — honestly diagnosing what's not working versus making excuses or quietly hoping you don't notice — is one of the clearest signals of how they'll actually behave once you're a paying client rather than a prospect.

What a strong answer looks like. A description of a defined process for reviewing underperformance: what data they look at, how quickly they typically identify an issue, and what kinds of adjustments they'd consider (creative changes, audience changes, offer changes) before recommending simply increasing budget.

What should prompt further investigation. An answer suggesting results are effectively guaranteed, or one that immediately blames external factors (the market, the algorithm, your product) without describing any process for actually diagnosing and adjusting the campaign itself.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. Whether there's a defined review point (for example, after the first 30 days) where performance is formally assessed and a joint decision made about next steps.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming a lack of immediate results after the first week or two automatically means the agency or strategy has failed. Many campaigns, particularly ones involving any organic component or a longer sales cycle, genuinely need more time before meaningful conclusions can be drawn — the useful thing to evaluate is whether the agency has a clear, honest process for assessing this, not whether results appeared instantly.

Question 7: Can I See Examples of Work for Businesses Like Mine?

Why this matters. Directly relevant examples reduce uncertainty about whether an agency's approach will actually translate to your specific business model, audience, and sales process — though this isn't the only factor that matters, since a strong generalist agency with a thorough process can also succeed outside their most common industries.

What a strong answer looks like. Specific, relevant work samples, ideally with some context about strategy and outcome, not just a polished final asset shown without explanation.

What should prompt further investigation. A portfolio consisting entirely of generic, visually impressive content with no explanation of the actual strategy or business results behind it, or reluctance to show any real client work at all, citing vague confidentiality without offering any anonymized alternative.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. A walkthrough of one or two specific projects, including what the goal was and what was actually delivered, rather than a static gallery of images.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming that a lack of exact industry match is disqualifying. A restaurant business doesn't necessarily need an agency that has only worked with restaurants — what matters more is whether the agency has handled comparable complexity (local audience targeting, high-frequency content needs, seasonal promotions) regardless of the specific industry label.

Question 8: What's the Minimum Commitment Period?

Why this matters. Contract length affects both your financial commitment and your flexibility if the relationship isn't working out. Understanding this clearly before signing avoids an unpleasant surprise if you need to exit earlier than expected.

What a strong answer looks like. A clear, specific commitment period (month-to-month, three months, six months, or annual), an honest explanation of why that length makes sense for the type of work involved, and clear terms for cancellation or notice period.

What should prompt further investigation. Pressure to sign a long-term contract immediately, before any working relationship has been established, or vague answers about cancellation terms that seem to shift depending on how the question is asked.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. The exact notice period required to cancel, and whether there are any exit fees or penalties for ending the relationship early.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming a longer minimum commitment is automatically a red flag. Some services — SEO, in particular, or any strategy that genuinely needs months to show results — reasonably require a longer minimum commitment to be effective, since short engagements sometimes don't allow enough time for the work to actually take hold. The distinction worth watching is whether the length is explained with a legitimate reason, versus being used mainly to lock in revenue regardless of performance.

Question 9: Who Owns the Accounts, Content, and Data?

Why this matters. This is one of the most important and most commonly overlooked questions on this list. If ownership of your ad accounts, social media pages, website access, creative files, and analytics data isn't explicitly clarified and confirmed in writing, you risk a genuinely difficult situation if you ever want to switch agencies or bring work in-house later — sometimes losing access to your own historical data, ad account, or creative assets entirely.

What a strong answer looks like. A direct, unambiguous confirmation that you own your ad accounts (set up under your own Meta Business Manager or equivalent, with the agency added as a partner rather than the account being created and owned by them), your social media pages, your website and domain, and all creative files and content produced for you — with this ownership structure explained clearly and reflected in the contract.

What should prompt further investigation. Any hesitation, vagueness, or resistance to confirming this clearly, or a setup where accounts are created and controlled entirely under the agency's own business assets rather than yours.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. Written confirmation in the contract of exactly who owns each asset — ad accounts, social pages, website, domain, hosting, creative source files, and any analytics or tracking setups — and what the process is for transferring full access to you if the relationship ends.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming this only matters if you're planning to leave the agency. Even if you have no intention of switching agencies anytime soon, clear ownership protects you against a range of situations — the agency closing down, a dispute, or simply wanting more direct visibility into your own accounts later — that are hard to predict in advance.

Practical example. A business that had its Facebook ad account created and owned entirely by their previous agency's Business Manager found themselves unable to access historical ad performance data or quickly resume campaigns when they switched to a new provider, because the account itself technically belonged to the agency, not the business — a situation entirely avoidable by confirming account ownership structure before the very first campaign was ever launched.

Question 10: Why Should We Work With You Specifically?

Why this matters. This closing question tests whether the agency has actually been listening and thinking about your specific business throughout the conversation, or whether they're running the same generic pitch with every prospect regardless of what you've told them.

What a strong answer looks like. A specific response that references details from your actual conversation — your particular goals, constraints, or challenges — rather than a generic list of the agency's general strengths that could apply to any prospective client.

What should prompt further investigation. A rehearsed-sounding answer focused entirely on the agency's awards, team size, or years in business, with no specific connection back to anything you've actually discussed about your own situation.

What to ask for in writing or in more detail. Nothing formal needed here — this is more about listening carefully to how specific and grounded the answer actually is in the moment.

Common misunderstanding. Assuming the most enthusiastic or confident-sounding answer is the best one. Genuine understanding sometimes sounds more measured than a purely enthusiastic pitch — an agency that says "here's specifically what we'd focus on first, and here's a challenge I'd flag honestly" is often showing more real understanding than one offering unqualified excitement with no specifics.

Understanding What Meaningful Reporting Actually Looks Like

Reporting expectations differ by service, and it's worth knowing roughly what a reasonable report should include so you can judge whether an agency's proposed reporting is actually meaningful or mostly surface-level.

For social media management, a reasonable report typically covers reach, engagement rate, content output (how much was actually published), audience growth over time, and — where measurable — clicks through to your website or WhatsApp, and any leads that can be attributed back to social activity.

For paid advertising, a reasonable report typically covers total spend, impressions, clicks, click-through rate (CTR), cost per click (CPC), number of leads or purchases generated, cost per lead or cost per acquisition, and, where genuinely applicable and properly tracked, return on ad spend (ROAS).

For website work, a reasonable report typically covers traffic trends, conversion or enquiry numbers, and specific technical improvements made (page speed, mobile usability, SEO fundamentals) rather than only a generic "the site looks better now" summary.

Across all of these, it's worth being clear-eyed about the difference between vanity metrics and business metrics. Follower count, likes, and impressions are easy to report and can look impressive, but they don't automatically translate into enquiries or sales — a page with a modest following that consistently generates real customer conversations is doing its job better than one with a large following and no measurable business impact. A good agency should be willing to discuss both kinds of metrics honestly, rather than leaning entirely on the numbers that look best in isolation.

Understanding Agency Pricing Without Assuming Cheaper or Pricier Is Better

Agency pricing varies enormously, and the variation usually reflects genuine differences in scope rather than one agency simply overcharging or undercharging. Cost is generally driven by which services are included, how much content is being produced each month, whether ad management is part of the scope (and how much ad spend is being managed), whether video, photography, or other production work is involved, whether website work is included, how many platforms are being managed simultaneously, how much strategic planning and reporting is built into the engagement, and how senior or specialized the actual team working on your account is.

Neither the cheapest nor the most expensive option is automatically the right choice. A very low-cost package might be entirely appropriate for a business that only needs light, consistent posting support — but the same low-cost package would likely be inadequate for a business needing serious ad management and conversion-focused strategy. A premium-priced agency might be genuinely worth it for a business with complex needs and enough budget to benefit from deeper strategic involvement — or might be significant overkill for a business that just needs steady, reliable execution. The useful question isn't "is this price good or bad" in isolation, but "does the scope and deliverable at this price genuinely match what my business actually needs right now."

What to Clarify About Ownership and Access Before Signing

Beyond the account-ownership question covered above, it's worth working through a broader checklist of access and ownership items before signing any agreement, since these details are easy to overlook amid excitement about getting started.

  • Social media accounts. Confirm you retain admin-level ownership, with the agency added as a manager or collaborator rather than being the primary account owner.
  • Meta Business assets. Confirm your Meta Business Manager, ad accounts, and Pages are owned under your own business, with the agency granted partner access rather than owning these assets themselves.
  • Ad accounts. Confirm specifically who owns the ad account and its historical data, and what happens to that history if you switch providers.
  • Website and domain. Confirm you own your domain registration and have full access to your hosting account, rather than these being registered under the agency's own accounts.
  • Creative and source files. Confirm you'll receive editable source files for design work (not just flattened final images), and raw or original footage for video work, not only finished edits.
  • Content produced for you. Confirm ownership of captions, graphics, and any other content created specifically for your brand.
  • Analytics and tracking setups. Confirm you have direct access to analytics platforms (Meta Pixel, Google Analytics, and similar) set up under your own accounts, rather than data being visible only through the agency's own dashboard.
  • Passwords and access credentials. Confirm a clear, documented process for how login credentials and access are shared, stored, and transferred if needed.
  • Intellectual property. Confirm, in the contract, that you own the intellectual property rights to content and creative produced specifically for your business once paid for.

Red Flags Across These Answers

A few patterns are worth taking seriously if they show up across multiple questions during your conversations, rather than being a single odd moment in an otherwise solid discussion.

QuestionGood Answer SignalRed Flag Signal
Past resultsSpecific, verifiable example with real contextVague claims, no specifics offered even when asked
Reporting approachMetrics tied clearly to your business goalsRelies entirely on vanity metrics like followers or likes
Team structureNames and roles of who actually does the workVague about who is assigned, no clear point of contact
Scope and pricingItemized deliverables, clear on what costs extraSingle vague number, undefined "ongoing" deliverables
Account/data ownershipDirect confirmation you retain full ownershipHesitation, vagueness, or accounts controlled by the agency
Contract termsClear commitment period and cancellation termsPressure to sign immediately, unclear exit terms

Beyond these specific answer patterns, a few broader behaviors are worth watching for across the whole conversation: guaranteed viral results or guaranteed follower counts (organic reach and growth can't be guaranteed by any legitimate agency), guaranteed search rankings for specific keywords (no one controls Google's algorithm), guaranteed lead volume with no qualification or context about budget and market conditions, unwillingness to explain their actual process in terms you can follow, and any use of pressure tactics — countdown-style urgency, "this price is only available today" — during what should be a considered business decision.

It's worth balancing this with some honesty: not every small agency or independent freelancer lacking a large, polished portfolio is automatically weaker than a bigger name. Some of the most attentive, effective work comes from smaller teams or individual specialists who simply haven't invested heavily in marketing themselves. The red flags above are about the substance and honesty of the answers you get, not about the size or fame of the agency giving them.

A Simple Pre-Hiring Checklist

Before signing anything, it's worth running through this list:

  • Asked all 10 questions above directly, in an actual conversation, not just relying on a written proposal
  • Received specific, verifiable examples of relevant past work, not just generic claims
  • Received a clear, itemized breakdown of what's included in the price and what would cost extra
  • Confirmed the specific communication and reporting cadence you can expect, in writing
  • Understood what happens if results underperform, and whether there's a defined review process
  • Confirmed the minimum commitment period and exact cancellation terms
  • Received explicit, written confirmation that you retain ownership of your accounts, content, and data
  • Compared answers to these same questions across at least two or three agencies, not just evaluated one in isolation
