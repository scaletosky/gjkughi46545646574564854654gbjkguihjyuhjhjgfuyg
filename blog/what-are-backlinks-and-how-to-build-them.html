What Are Backlinks and How to Build Them

A clear understanding of backlinks plus realistic, ethical tactics a small business can actually execute.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 9 September 2026·18 min read

You've probably heard that backlinks matter for SEO, maybe from a friend, a course, or an agency pitch — but if you're not sure what a backlink actually is, or how a small business with no existing online authority is supposed to get any, you're not alone. Most explanations either assume you already understand the basics or jump straight to generic advice like "build high-quality backlinks" without saying how.

Here's the short version: a backlink is simply a link from another website to yours, and search engines treat it as one signal — among many — of trust and relevance. Backlinks still matter in 2026, but they're not a magic ranking formula, and chasing large numbers of low-quality links can actively hurt you. This guide explains what backlinks actually are, why they matter, what makes one link more valuable than another, and realistic, ethical ways a small business can start building them without a big budget or existing authority.

What Is a Backlink, in Plain Terms

A backlink — also called an inbound link or external link pointing to your site — is a hyperlink on someone else's website that leads to a page on yours. If a local news site writes an article about small businesses in your city and includes a link to your website, that's a backlink.

This is different from an internal link, which is a link from one page to another page within your own website — for example, linking from your homepage to your services page. Internal links help with site structure and navigation, but they're not backlinks, because they don't come from an external, independent website.

Think of a backlink loosely like a vote of confidence. If several independent, reputable websites link to your page, that's a signal to search engines that other sources consider your content worth referencing — similar in spirit to how a recommendation from someone credible carries more weight than a recommendation from a stranger.

Why Backlinks Still Matter for SEO in 2026

Search engines aim to show the most relevant, trustworthy results for a given search. Content quality and on-page relevance are central to that, but they're not the only signal used — search engines also look at signals that come from outside your own website, and backlinks are one of the clearest examples of that kind of external signal.

The logic is fairly intuitive: if your content only claims to be good, that's less convincing than if other independent websites are actually linking to it because they find it useful, credible, or worth referencing. This is part of why backlinks have remained relevant for as long as they have, even as search engines have gotten more sophisticated about understanding content quality directly.

That said, backlinks are one part of a larger picture. Relevant, well-structured content, a technically sound website, good user experience, and content that genuinely matches what searchers are looking for all matter alongside backlinks — not instead of them. A site with excellent backlinks but thin, unhelpful content is unlikely to rank well, and a site with wonderful content but zero backlinks and poor technical SEO may also struggle. Backlinks work best as one piece of a broader, genuinely sound SEO approach, not as a standalone fix.

Not All Backlinks Are Equal: Quality vs Quantity

This is probably the single most important thing to understand before doing any backlink outreach: a large number of low-quality links is not better than a smaller number of genuinely relevant, high-quality ones. In many cases, chasing raw quantity can actively work against you.

What Makes a Backlink High Quality

A few factors generally make one backlink more valuable than another:

  • Relevance — a link from a website in your industry or local area is generally more meaningful than a link from a completely unrelated site. A link to your bakery from a local food blog is more relevant than one from an unrelated tech forum.
  • Authority — a link from a well-established, trusted, reputable site generally carries more weight than one from a brand-new or low-quality site with little history.
  • Context — a link that appears naturally within relevant content — for example, mentioned in an article about local businesses — tends to matter more than a link dropped into an unrelated list with no real context.
  • Anchor text — this is the clickable text of the link itself. Natural, varied anchor text (your business name, a relevant phrase, or "click here" style text) tends to look more genuine than the exact same keyword-stuffed phrase repeated across every link pointing to your site.

Follow, Nofollow, Sponsored, and UGC Links

Not every link is treated identically by search engines. Website owners can add attributes to a link that signal its nature: a nofollow attribute traditionally signaled that a site shouldn't pass authority through that particular link, a sponsored attribute flags a paid or promotional link, and a UGC (user-generated content) attribute flags links coming from things like blog comments or forum posts. In practice, links with these attributes can still send some referral traffic and visibility even if their direct SEO weight is limited, so they're not worthless — they're just generally treated differently from a standard, editorially-given link.

Why One Relevant Link Can Outweigh Dozens of Poor Ones

A single link from a genuinely relevant, respected local publication or industry site can realistically do more for your site's credibility, in the eyes of both search engines and actual human readers, than dozens of links from irrelevant directories or low-quality sites most people have never heard of. This is the core reason quality-first thinking should guide your entire backlink strategy from the start.

Realistic Backlink Sources for a Small Business

Generic SEO advice often assumes you already have some authority or a marketing budget for outreach campaigns. Here are backlink sources that are genuinely realistic for a small business starting from close to zero.

Local Business Directories and Associations

Local chambers of commerce, local business associations, and reputable regional directories are often one of the most accessible starting points. These aren't glamorous, but they're legitimate, relevant to your location, and relatively easy to get listed on — many simply require an application or membership.

Partnerships With Complementary (Non-Competing) Local Businesses

A wedding photographer and a wedding venue aren't competitors, but they serve overlapping customers. Cross-mentioning each other on your respective websites — a "recommended partners" or "vendors we work with" section — is a natural, mutually beneficial way to earn a relevant local backlink.

Guest Contributions on Relevant Industry Blogs

Writing a genuinely useful guest article for a blog in your industry, with a natural link back to your site in your author bio or within the content where it fits contextually, can earn a strong, relevant backlink — provided the content actually adds value to that publication's audience rather than reading as a thinly disguised advertisement.

Local Press and "Best of [City]" Roundups

Local news outlets and lifestyle publications regularly write roundup articles — "Best Cafes in [City]," "Top Local Services for [Category]." Reaching out with a genuine pitch, especially if you have something newsworthy (an event, a milestone, a community initiative), can earn you a mention and a link in exactly this kind of content.

Supplier and Vendor Relationships

If you work with suppliers, manufacturers, or vendors who list their retailers or partners on their own websites, ask whether you can be included with a link back to your site. This is often an easy, overlooked opportunity since the relationship already exists.

Event Participation and Community Organizations

Sponsoring a local event, participating in a community initiative, or being a member of an industry association often comes with a listing on that organization's website — another naturally relevant, legitimate backlink opportunity that arises from something you're likely already doing.

Digital PR-Lite: Getting Noticed Without a PR Budget

"Digital PR" often sounds like something only larger companies with dedicated budgets can do, but a scaled-down version is realistic for small businesses too.

Offering Expert Quotes to Journalists and Bloggers

Local journalists and industry bloggers frequently need quotes or expert commentary for articles they're writing. Making yourself available as a knowledgeable source in your industry — responding promptly and helpfully when opportunities arise — can earn you a mention and a link, and builds a relationship for future opportunities too.

Creating One Genuinely Useful, Link-Worthy Resource

Rather than trying to build many separate linkable assets, focus on creating one genuinely excellent resource — a detailed local guide, an original survey or dataset relevant to your industry, a genuinely comprehensive how-to guide — something other sites in your space would naturally want to reference because it's useful, not because you asked them to link to it.

What NOT to Do: Risky and Outdated Backlink Tactics

Some tactics that were once common are now genuinely risky, and understanding why matters as much as knowing what to avoid.

Buying Backlinks in Bulk

Paying for large quantities of backlinks from low-quality or unrelated sites violates most search engines' guidelines and carries real penalty risk — including the possibility of your site's rankings dropping significantly if this pattern is detected. The apparent shortcut isn't worth the risk to a business's long-term visibility.

Private Blog Networks (PBNs)

A PBN is a network of low-quality websites created specifically to link to each other and to client sites, artificially inflating link counts. These networks get detected and penalized regularly, and any business relying on them risks losing rankings entirely when that happens.

Irrelevant Directory Spam

Submitting your site to hundreds of low-quality, irrelevant directories — many of which exist purely to sell listings with no real audience — tends to add little value and can look unnatural if it's a large share of your overall backlink profile.

Mass, Generic Outreach

Sending the same generic "please link to my site" email to hundreds of website owners, with no personalization or genuine relevance to their site, has a very low success rate and can damage your reputation with the sites you're reaching out to for future opportunities.

Automated Link-Building Tools

Tools that promise to automatically generate large numbers of backlinks with no manual review are almost always producing low-quality, spammy links that can do more harm than good to your site's credibility.

Over-Optimized, Repetitive Anchor Text

Using the exact same keyword-heavy anchor text across every backlink pointing to your site looks unnatural to search engines, since real, organic link-building produces varied, natural-sounding anchor text rather than the same repeated phrase everywhere.

How to Check Your Current Backlinks

Before building new backlinks, it's worth understanding what you already have — many small businesses have a few existing backlinks they don't even know about.

Google Search Console is a free tool that includes a Links report, showing which external sites currently link to your website. This is a genuinely useful starting point since it costs nothing and comes directly from Google. There are also paid third-party tools that offer more detailed backlink analysis, competitor comparisons, and link quality scoring, though these come with a monthly cost that may not be necessary for a small business just getting started with the basics.

Checking your existing backlinks periodically also helps you spot problems early — for example, if a site you're linked from removes the page, or if you notice suspicious, spammy-looking links you didn't create yourself, which occasionally happens through no fault of your own.

Evaluating the Backlinks You Already Have

Once you can see your current backlinks, it's worth spending a little time actually looking through them rather than just noting the total count. Ask yourself, for each one: is this site relevant to my industry or location? Does it look like a genuine, maintained website with real content and real visitors? Is the link sitting in a natural, contextual spot, or does it look like it was dropped into a generic list with no real relevance?

Most small businesses find their existing backlink profile is a mix — a few genuinely good, relevant links (often from local directories they signed up for early on, or a press mention they'd forgotten about) alongside a handful of low-value or irrelevant ones that accumulated without much thought. Understanding this mix helps you decide where to focus new effort, and occasionally reveals a relationship or listing worth following up on further.

Practical Examples From Real Small-Business Situations

Abstract advice is harder to act on than seeing how it plays out for businesses similar to yours. A few realistic scenarios:

A local plumber gets listed on their city's official business directory and, separately, is included in a "Best Home Services in [City]" roundup published by a local lifestyle blog after the owner reached out directly with a short, genuine pitch mentioning their years in business and a couple of recent projects. Neither link required payment — just a bit of outreach effort and a directory application.

A boutique clothing brand sends a few of their new pieces to a local fashion blogger for an honest review, with no strings attached beyond asking that the blogger link back to the brand's site if they choose to feature it. The blogger writes a genuine feature, including a backlink, because the content was useful to their own audience — not because the brand paid for a link placement.

A small accounting firm writes a genuinely detailed guide on a tax topic relevant to a specific local industry — something more thorough than what's readily available elsewhere — and a regional business association later links to it as a resource for their members, because it's simply the best explanation available on that specific topic.

A neighborhood gym partners with a nearby physiotherapy clinic, a nutritionist, and a sports equipment shop — none of them direct competitors — to create a simple "local partners" page, with each business linking to the others. This kind of arrangement is easy to set up, genuinely useful for customers looking for related services, and produces multiple relevant local backlinks from one round of outreach.

A software company serving a specific regional market offers to write a genuinely useful guest post for an industry association's blog, focused on a real problem their target customers face, with a natural link back to their site included in the author bio — earning both a relevant backlink and direct exposure to a highly targeted audience.

Researching What Competitors Are Doing

Looking at where your direct competitors' backlinks come from — using either free tools with limited access or a paid backlink analysis tool — can reveal realistic opportunities you hadn't considered: a directory they're listed in that you're not, a local publication that's featured them, or an industry association they belong to. This isn't about copying their exact links one-for-one, but about identifying the types of legitimate sources that are realistically achievable in your specific industry and location.

How to Evaluate Whether a Specific Backlink Opportunity Is Worth Pursuing

Not every opportunity to get a link is worth your time, and being selective is actually part of doing this well rather than a limitation.

Ask Whether the Site Would Be Relevant to a Real Customer

If a genuine potential customer of yours would plausibly visit that website for a reason connected to your industry or location, that's a good sign. If the site has no realistic overlap with your actual audience, the link is unlikely to be worth much even if it's technically available.

Look at Whether the Site Actually Has Real Visitors

A website with almost no real traffic, no genuine content of its own, and seemingly no real audience is unlikely to pass much value, regardless of what it claims about its own authority. A quick look at the site's actual content — does it look genuinely maintained and read by real people — tells you a lot.

Consider Whether You'd Be Comfortable With the Association

Would you be comfortable if a customer saw your business listed or mentioned on that specific site? If the site's other content or associations feel off-brand, low-quality, or spammy, that discomfort is worth listening to — not every link is one you actually want, even if it's freely offered.

Check Whether It Requires Ongoing Payment for Placement

A one-time payment for a genuinely relevant, editorially-reviewed feature — for example, sponsoring a local event that comes with a mention — is different from an ongoing "pay for placement" scheme with a low-quality site that exists mainly to sell links. The former is a normal part of doing business locally; the latter carries the penalty risk discussed earlier.

How Backlinks Fit Into Your Broader SEO Effort

It's worth repeating, because it's easy to lose sight of once you start focusing on link building specifically: backlinks are one part of SEO, not the whole picture. A site can have a genuinely strong backlink profile and still rank poorly if the on-page content doesn't actually answer what searchers are looking for, the site is slow or difficult to use on mobile, or basic technical SEO issues — like missing title tags or a confusing site structure — haven't been addressed.

A more realistic way to think about it: strong content and solid on-page and technical SEO create the foundation that makes backlinks actually effective, while backlinks add credibility and authority on top of that foundation. Neither one compensates fully for a serious weakness in the other. If you're just getting started with SEO more broadly, it's worth working through on-page fundamentals and basic technical health alongside, not instead of, your backlink efforts.

Common Backlink Mistakes

A few mistakes come up repeatedly, especially for businesses new to backlink building.

  • Chasing volume over relevance. A large number of unrelated, low-quality links rarely helps, and can occasionally hurt, more than a handful of genuinely relevant ones.
  • Buying links from unrelated, low-quality sites. The short-term appeal of a quick purchase isn't worth the real penalty risk involved.
  • Never checking your existing backlink profile. You can't build a sensible strategy, or catch problems, without first knowing where you currently stand.
  • Spamming irrelevant directories. Submitting to every directory that will accept you, regardless of relevance or quality, adds little value and can look unnatural.
  • Expecting fast results. Backlink building, like most SEO work, tends to show its effects gradually over months, not days or weeks.
  • Using the same exact anchor text everywhere. Natural link profiles have varied anchor text, not one repeated exact-match phrase.

Local Indian Business Opportunities Worth Knowing About

For businesses operating in India, a few backlink sources are worth calling out specifically, since they're often more accessible than generic global advice suggests.

Local Business Associations and Trade Bodies

Many Indian cities have local trade associations, industry bodies, and chambers of commerce that maintain member directories on their websites. Joining a relevant association — something many businesses do anyway for networking and credibility reasons — often comes with a listing and a link as a natural side benefit.

Regional and City-Specific Publications

Beyond national news outlets, many Indian cities have local publications, city-specific lifestyle blogs, and regional business journals that regularly cover local businesses, openings, and community stories. These tend to be more accessible for a genuine, well-pitched local business story than large national publications, and the resulting backlink is highly relevant to a local audience.

Supplier and Distributor Networks

In many Indian industries — textiles, food and beverage, electronics retail, and others — supplier and distributor relationships are common, and many suppliers maintain a "where to buy" or dealer locator page on their website. If you already have this kind of relationship, asking to be included is a low-effort, highly relevant backlink opportunity.

Event and Exhibition Participation

Trade fairs, local exhibitions, and community events are common across Indian cities, and event organizers frequently list participating businesses and sponsors on their event website. Participating in relevant local events, something many small businesses already do for direct customer engagement, often produces a legitimate backlink as a secondary benefit.

Anchor Text: Getting the Details Right

Anchor text — the visible, clickable text of a link — deserves a closer look because it's an area where small businesses often either overthink it or ignore it entirely.

In natural, organic link building, anchor text tends to vary quite a bit: sometimes it's your business name, sometimes a general phrase like "this local bakery" or "read more here," and sometimes it's a more specific, relevant phrase describing what the linked page is about. This natural variation happens because real people writing real content don't think in terms of exact-match keyword phrases — they write however feels natural in the context of their own article.

When you're in a position to suggest anchor text yourself — for example, when writing a guest post or asking a partner business how they'd like to link to you — it's worth resisting the urge to always request the exact same keyword phrase. A more natural mix, including your business name and varied descriptive phrases, tends to look more genuine and is generally a safer, more sustainable approach than heavily repeated exact-match anchor text across every link you can influence.

Setting Realistic Expectations

It's worth being direct about something that gets oversold constantly in SEO marketing: there is no specific number of backlinks that guarantees a first-page ranking, and any claim promising a guaranteed ranking position through a set number of links should be treated with real skepticism. Search engines use many signals together, backlinks are just one of them, and competition levels vary enormously by industry and keyword.

What's realistic is this: consistent, genuine effort toward earning relevant backlinks, combined with solid content and technical SEO, tends to improve a site's overall authority and visibility over time. It's a compounding process, similar to reputation-building in the real world — gradual, cumulative, and best pursued as an ongoing practice rather than a one-time task you finish and forget about.

A Simple Backlink-Building Starter Plan

If you're starting from close to zero, here's a realistic, low-budget way to begin.

Source TypeExampleTypical Difficulty
Local directories/associationsChamber of commerce, local business directoryEasy — often just requires signup or a small membership
Complementary business partnershipsWedding photographer and venue cross-linkingEasy to moderate — requires reaching out and building the relationship
Supplier/vendor listingsBeing listed as a retailer on a supplier's siteEasy — relationship often already exists
Guest posts on industry blogsWriting a useful article for a relevant blogModerate — requires quality writing and a genuine pitch
Local press / "best of" roundupsBeing featured in a local news or lifestyle roundupModerate — requires a compelling pitch or newsworthy angle
Original resource or dataA genuinely useful guide or small original surveyHarder — requires real time investment upfront, but can pay off repeatedly

Step 1: Check Your Current Backlinks

Set up Google Search Console if you haven't already, and review the Links report to understand your current starting point.

Step 2: List 10 Realistic Local or Industry Targets

Write down ten specific, realistic websites, directories, or publications relevant to your business and location — not an abstract wish list, but sites you could genuinely reach out to or apply to this month.

Step 3: Submit to 3–5 Legitimate Local Directories

Start with directories and associations genuinely relevant to your location and industry, avoiding low-quality, generic directories that exist purely to sell listings with little real traffic or credibility.

Step 4: Reach Out to 2–3 Complementary Businesses

Identify businesses that share your customer base without competing directly, and propose a simple, mutually beneficial cross-link arrangement.

Step 5: Create One Genuinely Useful, Shareable Resource

Invest real time into one piece of content — a thorough guide, an original bit of research, or a genuinely comprehensive resource — that other relevant sites would plausibly want to reference on its own merit.

Step 6: Track What You've Done and What's Working

Keep a simple record of which outreach efforts led to actual links, so you can focus more of your future time on the approaches that are genuinely working for your specific business and industry.
