How Much Does It Cost to Build a Mobile App in 2026

A clear understanding of cost drivers and realistic ranges (not fabricated exact figures) so they can budget sensibly.

There's no single honest number for what it costs to build a mobile app in 2026 — anyone who gives you one figure without asking about your app first is either guessing or selling you something. What there is, is a clear set of factors that predictably push cost up or down: how many features and screens your app needs, whether you build native apps for each platform or a single cross-platform codebase, how custom the design needs to be, how complex the backend has to be, and who actually builds it — a freelancer, a small agency, or a larger development team. Understanding those factors gives you a realistic budget range for your specific app, which is far more useful than a headline number pulled from a generic guide.

This article is for founders and business owners in the early planning stages of a mobile app project, trying to work out what a "reasonable" budget looks like before talking to developers. It walks through the main cost drivers, rough complexity tiers you can use to place your own idea, how platform choice affects cost, the ongoing costs that continue after launch, and how to get an accurate quote once you're ready to move forward — plus the mistakes that most commonly blow up an app budget partway through.

Why There's No Single Answer (And What Drives the Range)

App development cost estimates you'll find online span an enormous range — from roughly ₹50,000 for a very basic single-screen app up to ₹50–80 lakh or more for a complex, feature-rich platform. That range isn't inconsistency between sources; it genuinely reflects how differently two things both called "an app" can be built.

A simple appointment-booking app for one salon and a multi-vendor marketplace app connecting hundreds of sellers with buyers are both, technically, "mobile apps." But one might need a few screens and a lightweight backend, while the other needs user accounts for multiple types of users, payment processing, real-time notifications, an admin dashboard, and infrastructure that can handle real transaction volume. The cost difference between them isn't small — it's often a difference of 10x or more.

The factors that create this spread are fairly consistent across most estimates: complexity (number of features and how technically demanding each one is), platform choice (native vs cross-platform, one platform vs both), design requirements (template-based vs fully custom), backend complexity (a simple content app vs one handling real-time data, payments, or complex business logic), and who builds it (freelancer, small agency, or larger development team, each at different price points). Understanding each of these individually is what turns a vague "how much does an app cost" question into a specific, answerable one for your particular project.

The Main Cost Drivers

Number of features and screens. This is usually the single biggest driver. Every additional screen, every additional user flow, and every additional feature (login, search, payments, chat, notifications, and so on) adds development, design, and testing time. A five-screen app with basic functionality will always cost meaningfully less than a twenty-screen app with several interactive features, regardless of who builds either one.

Native vs hybrid vs cross-platform. Native apps are built separately for iOS and Android using each platform's own tools and language, generally giving the best performance and platform-specific feel, but requiring two separate builds. Cross-platform frameworks (like Flutter or React Native) let you build largely one codebase that runs on both platforms, which is typically faster and cheaper upfront — often costing meaningfully less than building two fully separate native apps — though very performance-intensive apps (heavy gaming, advanced camera or AR features) sometimes still favor native for that reason.

Design complexity. A template-based design using largely standard UI components costs less than a fully custom design system with bespoke animations, illustrations, and interaction patterns. Design isn't purely cosmetic either — thoughtful UX directly affects how well users actually complete the actions your app is built around, which is part of why it's not the area most worth cutting corners on to save cost.

Backend and data complexity. A simple app that mostly displays content or takes basic form submissions needs a fairly lightweight backend. An app handling user accounts, real-time data, payment processing, complex business logic, or integrations with multiple external services needs meaningfully more backend engineering — and this is one of the areas where costs can expand quickly if the scope isn't clearly defined upfront.

Team type. A freelancer typically offers the lowest cost but the least redundancy — if they're unavailable, your project stalls, and quality varies widely by individual. A small agency generally offers a fuller team (design, development, QA) at a mid-range cost. A larger development company or team typically costs more but usually brings more process, more redundancy, and more experience handling complex or larger-scale projects.

It's worth being explicit here: exact pricing for your specific app requires a real project quote based on your actual requirements. Anyone offering a precise number before understanding your features, platform choice, and backend needs is estimating from very limited information.

Native vs Hybrid: How This Choice Affects Cost

Choosing between native and cross-platform development is one of the earlier, more consequential decisions in shaping your budget. Native development — building separately in Swift/Kotlin for iOS and Android respectively — generally costs more upfront because you're effectively building and maintaining two applications, even if they share the same design and functionality. It tends to offer the best raw performance and the most native-feeling user experience, which matters more for some app types (heavy graphics, camera-intensive features, complex animations) than others.

Cross-platform development lets a single codebase run on both platforms with relatively minor platform-specific adjustments, which typically reduces both development cost and time compared to two full native builds. For most business apps — content-driven apps, booking systems, e-commerce, service apps — the performance difference between a well-built cross-platform app and a native one is negligible to the end user, which is why cross-platform has become the default choice for a large share of business apps built in 2026, particularly for a first version.

This decision connects closely to whether you're planning to launch on one platform or both from day one, which is worth thinking through together rather than separately.

One Platform First or Both at Once?

Launching on a single platform first — typically Android in the Indian market, given its dominant share of smartphone users here, though this can shift depending on your specific target audience — is a common and often sensible way to reduce initial cost and validate demand before committing to a full cross-platform (or dual native) build.

The trade-off is straightforward: launching on one platform first costs less initially and lets you test real user response before investing further, but it does mean leaving out users on the other platform for that initial period. Whether that trade-off makes sense depends on where your specific target audience actually is — a B2B tool aimed at a business audience with a high proportion of iPhone users, for instance, might reasonably prioritize iOS first despite Android's larger overall market share, while a mass-market consumer app targeting a broad Indian audience would more often start with Android.

If budget is the primary constraint, launching narrower — one platform, core features only — and expanding once you have real usage data is generally a more capital-efficient path than trying to launch everything at once and running out of budget before you find out whether the app actually resonates with users.

Hidden and Ongoing Costs Beyond the Build

One of the most common budgeting mistakes is treating the initial build cost as the total cost of having an app. In reality, a meaningful set of costs continues well after launch, and skipping this planning is a frequent source of unpleasant surprises.

  • App store fees. Apple charges an annual developer account fee; Google charges a one-time registration fee for Android. Neither is large individually, but both are recurring or required costs worth budgeting for from the start.
  • Hosting and backend infrastructure. Any app with a real backend needs ongoing server or cloud hosting, which scales with your user base and usage — a small early-stage app might cost relatively little monthly, while a growing app with a large active user base will cost proportionally more.
  • Maintenance and OS compatibility. Both iOS and Android release regular updates, and apps occasionally need adjustments to stay compatible, secure, and functional as the underlying operating systems evolve. Treating maintenance as optional tends to result in an app that quietly degrades or breaks over time.
  • Future feature additions. Almost no app ships in its final form. Budgeting only for version one, with no plan for iterating based on real user feedback, often means the app stalls exactly when it's starting to show promise.
  • Bug fixes and support. Even a well-built app surfaces issues once real users start using it in ways the original testing didn't fully anticipate — budgeting some ongoing capacity for this is more realistic than assuming a perfect launch.

A commonly cited industry rule of thumb is budgeting somewhere in the range of 15–20% of your initial development cost annually for ongoing maintenance — this varies by app complexity and usage, but it's a reasonable starting assumption for planning purposes rather than treating post-launch costs as an afterthought.

It's worth walking through a simple illustrative example of how this adds up over a typical first year. Imagine a mid-complexity business app with an initial build cost of roughly ₹6,00,000. Maintenance at 15–20% would put ongoing annual costs somewhere around ₹90,000 to ₹1,20,000, on top of separate hosting costs (which scale with usage, but might reasonably start in a modest monthly range for an early-stage app with a smaller user base) and app store fees. None of these individual figures is large on its own, but a founder who budgeted only the initial ₹6,00,000 and treated the project as "done" at launch is likely to feel the ongoing costs as an unplanned surprise rather than an expected part of running the app — which is exactly the gap this section is meant to help avoid.

This same logic applies whether the illustrative build cost is smaller or larger — the specific rupee figures will differ with project scope, but the proportional planning approach (build cost, plus a maintenance percentage, plus hosting, plus store fees) holds regardless of where your particular app falls on the complexity spectrum.

How Team Location Affects Cost

Where your development team is based has a real, often underappreciated effect on cost, largely because hourly rates differ substantially by region and experience level. Development teams in India generally offer meaningfully lower rates than teams in the US, UK, or Western Europe for comparable work — this is a widely recognized reason businesses worldwide outsource app development to Indian teams, and it's equally relevant for Indian businesses building for their own domestic market, since local teams typically combine those lower rates with a better understanding of local user behavior, payment preferences (UPI integration, for instance, is a near-standard requirement here that some international teams may be less familiar with), and language needs.

Within India itself, rates also vary somewhat by city and team size — established agencies in major tech hubs sometimes charge a premium over smaller teams or agencies in other cities, though this doesn't automatically translate to proportionally better output. As with the broader "cheapest vs best value" point elsewhere in this article, location and rate are worth weighing alongside portfolio, communication quality, and relevant experience rather than treated as the deciding factor on their own.

Cost by App Type: A Few Illustrative Examples

Beyond the general complexity tiers above, it's useful to see how a few common app categories tend to map onto that spectrum, since "what kind of app" is often the first real question a developer will ask once you request a quote.

A booking or appointment app for a single business — a salon, clinic, or service provider — typically sits in the simple-to-mid-complexity range: a calendar/scheduling interface, customer accounts, notifications, and sometimes basic payment collection. This is one of the more predictable, well-understood app types to scope and build.

A basic e-commerce app for a single brand selling a defined product catalog generally falls in the mid-complexity range — product listings, cart, checkout, payment integration, and order tracking are all fairly standard, well-established patterns, though the cost rises with catalog size, variant complexity, and how custom the design needs to be.

A content or community app — think a fitness app with workout content, or a community/forum-style app — varies more widely depending on whether content is static (lower cost) or requires a content management backend, user-generated content moderation, and social features like comments or likes (higher cost).

A marketplace or multi-vendor platform — connecting multiple sellers or service providers with buyers — is consistently one of the more expensive categories to build properly, because it requires accounts and dashboards for at least two different user types, payment splitting or escrow logic, discovery and search functionality, and often review/rating systems, on top of the standard app fundamentals.

A fintech or healthtech app handling sensitive financial or medical data sits at the higher end regardless of how simple the core feature set looks on the surface, because of the additional security, encryption, and regulatory compliance work required — this is an area where cutting corners on backend and security investment carries real risk beyond just user experience.

Why Two "Similar" Apps Can Cost Very Differently

A common source of confusion happens when a founder compares their planned app to an existing one and expects a similar price — "this should cost about the same as [well-known app]," without accounting for the fact that most successful apps have years of iteration, a large existing engineering team maintaining them, and infrastructure built to handle a scale most new apps won't need for a long time.

What you're actually paying for in an initial build is a first version — a genuinely solid, well-built MVP or early-stage product, not a feature-complete replica of an app that's been refined over several years by a much larger team. Comparing your budget to a mature app's current feature set, rather than to what that app looked like in its first version, is one of the more common ways founders end up with unrealistic budget expectations before even talking to a developer.

A related mismatch happens with feature requests borrowed loosely from multiple different apps — "the search from this app, the chat from that app, the payment flow from a third app" — each individually reasonable, but combined, representing a scope closer to several mature products merged together than a focused first version. Breaking your own request down feature by feature, and being honest about which ones are truly essential for launch versus aspirational for later, tends to produce both a more realistic budget and a better first product.

How to Get an Accurate Quote for Your Specific App

Once you're ready to move from research to real quotes, the accuracy of what you get back depends heavily on how much clarity you can provide upfront. A vague brief — "I want an app like Zomato but for my industry" — tends to produce either an equally vague quote or a wildly inaccurate one, because the developer is essentially guessing at scope.

What helps produce an accurate quote:

  • A clear list of core features you actually need for version one, separated from features you'd like eventually but don't need immediately
  • A rough sketch of the main user journeys — what does a user actually do, step by step, inside the app
  • Your platform preference (or openness to a recommendation) and any specific performance requirements
  • Any existing brand assets, reference apps you like the feel of, or design preferences
  • A general sense of your budget range, so proposals come back realistic rather than wildly over or under what you can spend

It's genuinely worth getting quotes from two or three developers or agencies rather than accepting the first one — not primarily to find the cheapest option, but because comparing how different teams scope the same brief tells you a great deal about their process, their questions, and how well they actually understood what you're trying to build. A team that asks sharp, specific questions about your business and users is usually a better sign than one that returns a quote within hours of a brief conversation.

Choosing the right development partner matters as much as the number on the quote — a lower price that comes with less experience, less communication, or lower code quality often ends up costing more once you factor in rework, delays, or a rebuild down the line.

Common Cost-Related Mistakes

Picking the lowest quote without comparing scope or quality. Two quotes that look similar on price can represent very different actual deliverables — different feature sets, different code quality standards, different levels of testing. Comparing scope line by line matters more than comparing the final number alone.

Forgetting ongoing maintenance and update costs. As covered above, this is one of the most common gaps in early app budgets, and it's almost always avoidable with a small amount of upfront planning.

Trying to build every possible feature in the first version. It's tempting to want the full vision built immediately, but a bloated first version costs more, takes longer to launch, and delays the point where you actually learn whether real users want what you've built. A focused MVP that launches sooner and gets real feedback is usually the more capital-efficient path.

Not clarifying code and design ownership upfront. Before signing any agreement, confirm explicitly who owns the final code, design files, and any accounts created during development (app store accounts, backend infrastructure). This should be a standard, clearly stated part of any contract — its absence is a red flag worth raising directly.

Adding scope mid-project without adjusting budget and timeline. "Just one more feature" requests are common once development is underway, and they're rarely free — even small additions can affect timeline and cost, particularly once the underlying architecture has already been built around the original scope. Treating scope changes as a deliberate, budgeted decision rather than a casual add-on avoids a lot of downstream frustration on both sides.

A Simple Budgeting Checklist

  • List your core, must-have features separately from nice-to-have features for a later version
  • Decide your platform strategy — native, cross-platform, one platform first, or both together
  • Get two to three detailed quotes and compare scope, not just the final price
  • Budget separately for ongoing maintenance — a reasonable starting assumption is 15–20% of build cost annually
  • Confirm code and design ownership terms before signing anything
  • Set aside contingency budget for reasonable scope adjustments once real development begins

Choosing Between a Simple MVP and a Fuller Build

For most founders and small businesses without existing app-development experience, starting with a focused MVP — the smallest version that genuinely tests your core idea with real users — tends to be the more sensible path, even when the eventual vision is much bigger. It reduces upfront cost and risk, gets you real user feedback faster, and gives you actual usage data to guide what to build next, rather than guessing at a full feature set before you know which parts users actually value.

The exceptions are cases where a genuinely minimal version wouldn't actually be usable or wouldn't fairly represent the core value — certain marketplace or two-sided platforms, for instance, sometimes need a baseline level of functionality on both sides to be meaningfully testable at all. In those cases, it's worth discussing directly with your development partner what the smallest version that still genuinely works looks like, rather than cutting scope arbitrarily.

A useful way to frame this decision, when it's unclear, is asking what specific question you're trying to answer with the first version. If the goal is validating whether people want the core service at all, the MVP can usually be smaller than founders initially assume. If the goal is closer to a full product launch with an existing, committed user base, a fuller build justified by real, known demand is a more defensible starting point than launching minimal and hoping usage follows.

A Note on Third-Party Integrations and Their Cost Impact

Most modern apps rely on third-party services rather than building every capability from scratch — payment gateways (Razorpay and similar providers are standard in the Indian market), SMS or OTP verification services, maps and location services, push notification providers, and cloud storage are all typically integrated rather than built in-house, since building any of these independently would be far more expensive and time-consuming than using an established, well-tested service.

Each integration adds some development and testing time — connecting the service, handling its edge cases, and testing it properly — but this is almost always far cheaper than the alternative of building equivalent functionality from scratch. Where integrations do meaningfully add to cost is when several are combined into a single complex flow (a checkout process combining payment, inventory checking, and order confirmation notifications all firing correctly together, for instance), since the integration work compounds with the complexity of coordinating multiple services rather than any single integration being expensive on its own.

When getting quotes, it's worth listing out the specific third-party services your app will realistically need, since this is one of the more concrete ways a developer can refine an estimate — a vague "it needs payments" versus a specific "Razorpay integration with support for UPI, cards, and wallets" gives a meaningfully clearer basis for an accurate quote.
