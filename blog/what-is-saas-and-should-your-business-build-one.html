What Is SaaS and Should Your Business Build One

A clear understanding of what SaaS actually requires (technically and business-wise) so they can honestly assess if it's the right path.

Key Takeaways

"We should just turn this into a SaaS" is a sentence that comes up a lot once a business has built some internal process, tool, or spreadsheet system that works well enough that someone starts wondering whether other businesses facing the same problem would pay for access to it too. It's a genuinely reasonable instinct, and SaaS businesses have created enormous value over the past couple of decades. But "SaaS" as a term gets thrown around with a lot less precision than the commitment it actually represents, and a fair number of businesses head down this path without a clear picture of what building and running one actually involves.

This article explains what SaaS actually means, how it differs from related but distinct things like a mobile app or custom software, what it genuinely takes to build and run one, and offers an honest framework for deciding whether it's the right direction for your specific business idea — rather than presenting SaaS as a simple, low-effort path to recurring revenue.

It's a decision worth getting right at the outset, since the difference between a well-validated SaaS idea backed by realistic operational planning and an untested one built purely on enthusiasm can mean the difference between a genuinely sustainable business and a year or more of development time spent on something the market was never actually going to pay for. The rest of this article works through that decision step by step, starting with what SaaS actually is before moving into what it takes to build one well.

None of this is meant to discourage the idea outright — SaaS has genuinely built some of the most valuable and durable companies of the last two decades, and for the right idea, with the right validation and commitment behind it, it remains a strong business model. The goal here is simply to replace the vague, optimistic version of "we should build a SaaS" with a clear-eyed understanding of what that actually involves, so the decision to pursue it (or not) is based on real information rather than an appealing but incomplete narrative.

What SaaS Actually Means

SaaS stands for Software as a Service, and at its core, it describes software that's delivered as a subscription, hosted centrally by the company that built it, and accessed by customers over the internet rather than installed and run locally on each customer's own device or server. Instead of buying a piece of software once and owning a copy of it, customers pay an ongoing fee — usually monthly or annually — for continued access to a product that the company hosts, maintains, and updates on its own infrastructure.

This delivery model is the defining characteristic, and it has a few important implications that flow directly from it. Because the software lives on the company's own servers rather than the customer's device, updates and improvements roll out to every customer simultaneously, without requiring anyone to manually install a new version. Because access is subscription-based rather than a one-time purchase, the business's revenue is tied to ongoing value delivery and customer retention, rather than a single upfront transaction. And because typically many customers share the same underlying platform (what's often called a "multi-tenant" architecture), the company can serve a large number of customers from one well-maintained codebase, rather than managing separate, customized installations for each one.

It's worth noting that "hosted centrally, accessed via subscription" doesn't mean every SaaS product looks or feels identical. Some SaaS products are broad, general-purpose tools used across many industries — a project management platform, for instance, useful to almost any kind of team. Others are highly specialized, built for a narrow, specific use case within one particular industry — sometimes called "vertical SaaS" — like software built specifically for managing appointments at veterinary clinics, or specifically for scheduling and invoicing in the events industry. Vertical SaaS products often face less direct competition than broad, general-purpose tools, precisely because they're solving a narrower problem deeply rather than a broad problem shallowly, though the total addressable market for a narrow vertical is correspondingly smaller too.

SaaS vs a Regular App or Custom Software

These terms get used loosely enough in everyday conversation that it's worth drawing clear lines between them, since they describe genuinely different things even though they overlap in places.

A mobile app refers to the delivery format — software built to run on a phone or tablet, typically downloaded from an app store. An app can be part of a SaaS product (many SaaS companies offer a mobile app alongside their main web-based product), but "app" and "SaaS" aren't synonyms. A simple utility app that a customer buys once and owns outright isn't SaaS, since there's no subscription and no centrally hosted service being accessed. Conversely, plenty of SaaS products are used entirely through a web browser and don't have a dedicated mobile app at all, at least not initially.

Custom software refers to software built specifically for one particular business's unique needs, typically not intended for resale to other companies. A business might commission custom software to handle its own internal inventory management, for example, built to its exact specifications and used only by that one company. SaaS, by contrast, is built to serve many customers from a shared, standardized platform, with a business model built around recurring subscription revenue from that customer base rather than a single upfront project fee from one client. It's entirely possible for custom software built for internal use to later become the foundation of a SaaS product, if a business decides the underlying tool has broader market value — but that's a deliberate transition, not something that happens automatically just because the internal tool works well.

One more useful distinction: a website with some interactive functionality — a booking form, a calculator, a customer portal — isn't automatically SaaS either, even though it's delivered through a browser like most SaaS products are. The defining line is whether the core offering is genuinely software functionality being sold as an ongoing service (project management, data processing, automation, analytics, and similar categories of actual software capability), versus a website that happens to include some interactive features in support of a different underlying business, like a service business or an e-commerce store. A booking system embedded on a salon's website supports the salon's actual business (providing haircuts and treatments); it isn't, by itself, the salon selling software as its product.

What It Actually Takes to Build and Run a SaaS Product

This is the section most worth reading carefully before getting excited about a SaaS idea, because the popular narrative around SaaS — "build it once, earn recurring revenue forever" — understates just how much ongoing commitment the model actually requires, both technically and operationally.

Ongoing Development, Not a One-Time Build

Unlike a piece of software built once and delivered as a finished product, a SaaS product is never really "done." Customers expect continued improvements, new features, and bug fixes for as long as they're paying a subscription, and competitors in almost any SaaS category are continuously shipping their own improvements too. This means development is an ongoing operational cost for as long as the product exists, not a one-time project expense that gets paid off and then stops.

Customer Support Infrastructure

A SaaS product with paying customers needs some real capacity to handle support requests, bug reports, and general questions, and this need grows — sometimes faster than expected — as the customer base grows. Even a genuinely well-built, intuitive product will generate support requests, and how quickly and well those get handled directly affects customer retention, since a subscription customer who feels unsupported can simply cancel far more easily than a customer who made a one-time purchase and has no ongoing relationship to maintain.

Hosting and Infrastructure That Scales

Because SaaS products are centrally hosted, the company bears ongoing infrastructure costs — servers, databases, bandwidth — that scale (roughly) with the number of active customers and how heavily they use the product. Underestimating this cost, or not architecting the product with reasonable scalability in mind from early on, can create real problems later, either through unexpectedly high hosting bills or through performance issues as customer numbers grow beyond what the original infrastructure was built to comfortably handle.

Security and Data Privacy Responsibilities

A SaaS company is holding and processing customer data on an ongoing basis, which comes with real security and privacy responsibilities that a simple one-time software sale doesn't carry in the same way. Depending on the type of data involved and where customers are located, this can include specific regulatory compliance obligations, and at minimum it requires genuine, ongoing attention to security practices, since a serious data breach can be existential for a young SaaS company's reputation and customer trust, not just an inconvenience to fix.

None of the four responsibilities above are meant to suggest SaaS is impossible for a smaller team or a solo founder to take on — plenty of successful SaaS products are run by very small teams, sometimes even one person, particularly in the early stages. The point is that these responsibilities exist regardless of team size, and a realistic plan for handling them — even if that plan is "I will personally handle support and monitor infrastructure closely while the customer base is small, and revisit this as it grows" — is meaningfully different from not having thought about them at all until a problem forces the issue.

The Business Side: Is There Real Demand?

The technical and operational commitments above only make sense to take on if there's genuine, validated demand for the product in the first place, and this is where a lot of SaaS ideas quietly fail before any code gets written at all — or, worse, after a significant amount of code has already been written and real money has already been spent.

Validating demand before building anything substantial means actually talking to real potential customers about the specific problem the product would solve, understanding how they currently handle that problem (even if the current solution is a messy spreadsheet or a manual workaround), and getting a genuine sense of whether they'd actually pay for a better solution — not just whether they'd say the idea "sounds interesting" in a casual conversation, which is a much weaker signal than people often assume. Some founders go further and validate demand through a waitlist, a landing page describing the product before it's built, or even a manually-delivered version of the service (sometimes called a "concierge MVP") where the founder personally does, by hand, what the software would eventually automate, just to confirm real people will pay for the outcome before investing in building the automated version.

It's worth being specific about why casual positive feedback is such a weak signal compared with these more rigorous validation approaches. People are generally polite, and telling a founder "that sounds like a great idea" costs the person giving that feedback nothing. Asking someone to actually pay something, join a waitlist with a real commitment attached, or use a manually-delivered version of the service consistently over a few weeks filters out the difference between genuine interest and polite encouragement in a way that a casual conversation simply can't. Founders who skip this step and rely purely on encouraging conversations often discover the gap between polite interest and real paying demand only after a product has already been built — which is a considerably more expensive place to discover it.

Understanding Subscription Pricing and Churn

Because SaaS revenue is recurring rather than one-time, two concepts become central to whether the business actually works financially: pricing and churn. Pricing needs to reflect genuine value delivered relative to what it costs to acquire and support a customer, and getting this wrong in either direction — pricing too low to sustainably cover support and development costs, or too high relative to the value customers actually perceive — can undermine an otherwise sound product.

Churn, meaning the rate at which existing customers cancel their subscriptions, is arguably the single most important health metric for a SaaS business, because high churn can quietly undermine growth even while new customer acquisition looks healthy on the surface. A business that acquires 100 new customers a month but loses 90 existing customers in that same period is in a fundamentally different — and much more precarious — position than one acquiring 100 and losing 10, even though both might show similar total customer counts in any given month's snapshot. Building a product genuinely good enough, and support responsive enough, to keep churn low is arguably as important to long-term SaaS success as the initial product idea itself.

Churn is also worth understanding in two related forms: customer churn (the percentage of paying customers who cancel in a given period) and revenue churn (the percentage of recurring revenue lost, which can differ from customer churn if the customers who leave tend to be smaller or larger accounts than average). Tracking both gives a more complete picture than either alone — a business might have a concerning number of small customers churning while retaining its largest, most valuable accounts well, which looks quite different depending on which metric you're watching most closely.

Common SaaS Business Models

SaaS pricing isn't one-size-fits-all, and the specific model chosen has real implications for both revenue predictability and how naturally it aligns with the value customers actually get.

Pricing ModelHow It WorksBest For
Flat/tiered subscriptionFixed monthly or annual fee, often with multiple tiers offering different feature sets or usage limitsPredictable products where usage doesn't vary wildly between customers
Usage-basedPrice scales with actual usage — API calls, storage, number of transactions processed, etc.Products where value delivered scales directly and clearly with usage
FreemiumA free tier with limited features, paid tiers unlocking more capabilityProducts that benefit from wide initial adoption and word-of-mouth growth
Per-seat/per-userPrice scales with the number of individual users on a customer's teamTeam-based tools where value grows with more people actively using it

Each model carries trade-offs. Flat subscription pricing is simple to understand and predict but can feel misaligned if usage varies dramatically between customers. Usage-based pricing aligns cost with value more precisely but can make revenue harder to forecast and can create customer anxiety about unpredictable bills. Freemium can build a large user base quickly, but converting free users into paying customers at a sustainable rate is a genuine challenge that a meaningful number of freemium products never fully solve. Per-seat pricing works well for team collaboration tools but can create friction if customers deliberately limit how many people they add as users specifically to control cost.

Do You Need a Technical Co-Founder or Can You Outsource Development?

Both paths are genuinely viable, and the right choice depends on the founder's own situation, risk tolerance, and long-term goals for the business rather than there being one universally correct answer.

A technical co-founder brings deep, ongoing product and engineering judgment directly into the founding team, without the founder having to pay external rates for every development decision or manage an outside relationship for core product work. The trade-off is that finding the right technical co-founder — someone with genuinely compatible skills, working style, and commitment level — can take significant time, and equity is typically split to reflect that partnership, meaning the non-technical founder gives up meaningful ownership in exchange for that in-house capability.

Outsourcing development to a team or agency avoids the search for a co-founder and the equity trade-off that comes with it, and can get a product built and launched faster in many cases, since an established development team already has the relevant skills and processes in place. The trade-off is ongoing cost (development doesn't stop being a cost after launch, as covered earlier) and the need for the founder to manage that outside relationship effectively — communicating product vision clearly, reviewing work, and making technical decisions without necessarily having deep technical expertise themselves. Many successful SaaS founders have built and scaled real businesses this way, but it requires being genuinely engaged in the product and technical decisions, not simply handing off a vague idea and hoping for the best.

A middle-ground option worth mentioning is bringing on a technical advisor or fractional technical lead rather than a full co-founder — someone with relevant expertise who provides ongoing guidance and review on technical decisions without the full-time commitment or equity stake a co-founder relationship typically involves. This can work well for founders who want more technical judgment involved than a purely outsourced relationship provides, without taking on the search and equity trade-offs of finding a full co-founder. It's not a substitute for either full option in every situation, but it's a reasonable middle path many early-stage SaaS founders overlook.

Go-to-Market: Getting the Product in Front of Real Customers

Building the product, even once it's genuinely validated and well executed, is only half the challenge. SaaS products still need a real go-to-market plan — a clear sense of how potential customers will actually find out the product exists and be convinced to try it, since even an excellent product with no visibility won't generate meaningful revenue on its own.

The specific channels that work best vary considerably depending on the product and target customer. Content marketing and search engine optimization can work well for products solving problems people actively search for solutions to. Direct outreach and relationship-building tend to matter more for higher-priced, B2B-focused SaaS products with a smaller number of larger potential customers. Paid advertising can accelerate growth for products with clear, provable unit economics, but it's risky to lean on heavily before those economics are actually validated with real, organic-acquired customers first. Whichever channel or combination of channels makes sense for a specific product, treating go-to-market as a core part of the plan from early on — not an afterthought to figure out once the product is built — tends to produce a meaningfully smoother path to real revenue.

Should Your Business Build a SaaS Product? A Decision Framework

Before committing to building a SaaS product, a few honest questions help clarify whether it's the right path. Is there a real, validated problem — one you've confirmed through actual conversations with potential customers, not just an assumption based on your own experience or a hunch? Can you genuinely commit to the ongoing development, support, and infrastructure responsibilities described above, not just the initial build? Do the unit economics make sense — can you realistically price the product to cover customer acquisition and support costs while still leaving room for profit, given what similar solutions in the market currently charge?

If the honest answers to these questions are encouraging, SaaS can be a genuinely strong business model — it's created enormous, durable value for many companies precisely because recurring revenue, once established with reasonably low churn, compounds in a way that one-time sales don't. If the answers reveal real gaps — demand that's more assumed than validated, a founder or team not genuinely prepared for years of ongoing product commitment, or economics that don't clearly work — those gaps are worth addressing or reconsidering before committing significant time and money to building the product.

Two Illustrative Examples

A business consultant who has developed a genuinely effective internal framework for helping clients with a specific type of planning process might consider turning that framework into a SaaS tool other consultants or businesses in the same industry could use directly. Before building anything, validating this idea would mean talking to several other consultants or businesses in that space, understanding whether they'd actually pay a recurring fee for a tool version of the framework rather than continuing to do it manually or hiring the original consultant directly — a genuinely different question than whether they find the framework itself valuable.

A founder considering a SaaS idea for small restaurant inventory management might validate demand by manually helping two or three real restaurants with their inventory tracking for a few weeks — using spreadsheets and direct communication rather than any software at all — specifically to confirm those restaurants would pay for a better, automated version of that same help before investing in building the actual software product. If restaurants aren't willing to pay for the manually-delivered version of the value, it's a meaningful signal worth taking seriously before assuming an automated version would fare any better.

Common Mistakes First-Time SaaS Founders Make

Building the full product before validating there's real paying demand. This is, by a wide margin, the most common and most costly mistake, since it risks months of development time and real money on an idea that hasn't been genuinely tested with real potential customers, often based on nothing more than the founder's own conviction that the idea is a good one.

Underestimating the ongoing commitment SaaS requires beyond the initial build. As covered throughout this article, development, support, infrastructure, and security responsibilities continue indefinitely, not just through an initial launch period, and treating the launch as the finish line rather than the starting point is a common source of later burnout or neglect.

Setting pricing without understanding customer acquisition cost and expected lifetime value. Pricing that doesn't account for what it actually costs to acquire and retain a customer can leave a SaaS business technically growing in customer count while quietly losing money on every new customer acquired — a trap that can go unnoticed for months without deliberately tracking these numbers.

Trying to build every feature before launching. Waiting to launch until a product feels "complete" delays real market feedback that would actually inform which features matter most to real customers, often resulting in significant development time spent on features that turn out to matter less than expected once real usage data comes in.

Ignoring go-to-market planning until after the product is built. A well-built product with no clear plan for how real customers will discover and start using it tends to sit unused regardless of its quality, and go-to-market strategy generally benefits from being worked out alongside product development, not treated as a problem to solve only once building is finished.

A Practical SaaS Starting Checklist

  • Validate the core problem with real potential customers before building anything substantial
  • Decide on a pricing and business model that reflects genuine value delivered
  • Honestly assess your (or your team's) capacity for ongoing development and support, not just the initial build
  • Consider a minimum viable version, or even a manually-delivered concierge version, before building the full automated product
  • Plan for infrastructure, security, and support needs from early on rather than treating them as later problems
  • Sketch a realistic go-to-market plan alongside product development, not after it

Deciding If SaaS Is the Right Path for Your Business

SaaS can be a genuinely strong business model, but it's a meaningfully different commitment from building a piece of software once and selling it — and it's worth going in with clear eyes about that difference rather than the simplified "build once, earn forever" version of the pitch that circulates casually. A validated problem, a realistic understanding of ongoing operational commitment, and honest unit economics are the foundation that determines whether a SaaS idea actually becomes a sustainable business or an expensive, unfinished experiment.
