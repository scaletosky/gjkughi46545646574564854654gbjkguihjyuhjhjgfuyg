How to Decide Your Facebook/Instagram Ads Budget

A clear, logical method for arriving at a starting budget based on their goals and margins, not a guessed number.

If you're about to run your first Facebook or Instagram ads, the budget question usually feels the most stressful part — too little feels pointless, too much feels like a genuine risk if it doesn't work. Most of what's floating around online doesn't help much either, because it tends to offer a single arbitrary number ("start with ₹500/day!") as if every business, product, and goal needed the same starting point.

This article walks through an actual method for arriving at a budget that fits your specific business, rather than a number copied from somewhere else. It's written for small business owners about to run their first paid campaigns, or those who've dabbled with a random small amount and aren't sure whether to keep going, stop, or spend more.

There's No Universal Number — Here's the Actual Method

The honest starting point is that there's no single rupee amount that's "right" for Facebook or Instagram ads in general. A budget that makes complete sense for a high-margin e-commerce brand selling a ₹4,000 product could be wildly wrong for a local service business with a ₹300 average ticket size, and vice versa. Any article or ad claiming a universal starting number is skipping the part that actually matters.

What determines a sensible budget isn't a rule of thumb — it's your own numbers: what a customer is actually worth to you, what you can afford to spend to acquire one, and how much you need to spend to gather enough data to know whether a campaign is even working. The rest of this article walks through that method step by step, with illustrative ₹ examples throughout — treat every number as an example of the reasoning, not a figure to copy directly into your own campaign.

Step 1: Know Your Numbers Before You Set a Budget

Before opening Ads Manager, it's worth spending a few minutes working out two numbers about your own business, because everything else in this article builds on them.

Your average order value or customer value. This is roughly how much a typical customer is worth to you — either per purchase, or across their likely relationship with your business if customers tend to come back. A bakery selling a ₹1,200 average custom order has a very different number to work with than a salon whose average client is worth ₹2,500 over several visits a year, or a SaaS product whose customer might be worth ₹15,000 over a year of subscription.

Your rough acceptable cost-per-lead or cost-per-sale, based on your margin. This follows directly from the first number. If your product has healthy margins and a customer is worth ₹2,000 to you, spending ₹300 to acquire that customer through ads might be very reasonable. If your margins are thin and a customer is only worth ₹400 to you, that same ₹300 acquisition cost would eat most or all of your profit on the sale.

For a lead-generation business (a service business where the ad's job is to generate an inquiry, not a direct sale), this calculation needs one more step: your lead-to-customer conversion rate. If you close 1 in 5 leads, and a customer is worth ₹5,000 to you, then a lead itself is roughly worth ₹1,000 to you in expected value — which tells you how much you could reasonably afford to spend acquiring each lead and still come out ahead.

None of this needs to be precise on day one — a rough, honest estimate is enough to start with. The point of this step isn't spreadsheet-level accuracy, it's simply having some grounded reference point before picking a budget, rather than picking a number out of thin air.

It's also worth thinking briefly about your campaign objective before setting a budget, since Meta's system optimizes delivery differently depending on what you tell it to aim for. A campaign optimized for website traffic will chase clicks; one optimized for leads will chase form completions; one optimized for sales (with proper conversion tracking set up) will chase actual purchases. Setting a budget without first confirming the objective matches what you actually want the campaign to achieve is a common early mistake — a technically well-funded campaign optimized for the wrong outcome will dutifully deliver plenty of that wrong outcome, whether or not it's useful to the business.

Step 2: Separate Testing Budget from Scaling Budget

One of the most useful mental models for a first-time advertiser is treating your budget in two distinct phases rather than one continuous spend: a smaller testing phase to find out what actually works, followed by a larger scaling phase once you have real evidence a campaign performs.

The testing phase exists to answer specific questions: which audience responds, which ad creative gets attention, which offer or message actually converts. It's not meant to be profitable on its own — it's meant to be informative. Treating it as a learning cost rather than expecting immediate strong returns tends to lead to much better decisions than judging a campaign's viability from three days of data on a tiny budget.

This distinction matters because a common and understandable mistake is judging results too early on a budget that was never large enough to produce meaningful data in the first place. If you spend ₹200 total and get zero conversions, that tells you almost nothing — it could mean the offer doesn't work, or it could simply mean you haven't generated enough impressions and clicks yet for the algorithm to have found the right audience or for any real pattern to emerge. Separating "am I testing" from "am I scaling" in your own head helps avoid drawing conclusions from data that isn't actually conclusive yet.

Step 3: A Simple Formula to Start With

With your numbers from Step 1 in hand, here's a workable way to translate them into an actual starting budget.

First, decide a realistic monthly test budget — an amount you can genuinely afford to spend on learning, even if it doesn't produce a profitable return in that first month. This isn't money you're "risking" carelessly; it's a deliberate, capped learning investment, similar to how you might budget for any other business experiment.

Second, rather than committing that entire amount as one large daily spend from day one, spread it across a defined testing period — commonly two to three weeks — so you have enough total spend and enough time for Meta's delivery system to gather meaningful data, without either running out of budget too fast or spending it so slowly that you never gather enough signal to learn anything.

As an illustrative example only: a local business deciding it can comfortably set aside ₹6,000 for a first month of testing might spread that across roughly 20 days at ₹300/day, rather than spending it all in the first three days or dragging it out so thin (₹10/day) that no single day generates enough data to mean anything. The specific split depends entirely on your own numbers from Step 1 — this is an illustration of the reasoning, not a number to copy.

One detail worth understanding about how Meta actually spends a daily budget: it's treated as an average, not a strict daily cap. Meta's delivery system can spend meaningfully more than your set daily budget on a day it identifies as a high-opportunity day, and less on a slower day, balancing out to roughly your target average across the week. This is a normal part of how the system paces spend to find opportunities, not a sign something's gone wrong if a single day's spend looks higher than expected.

Daily Budget vs Lifetime Budget: Which to Choose

Meta Ads Manager asks you to choose between two budget types when setting up a campaign or ad set, and picking the right one for your situation is a small decision that's worth understanding rather than defaulting to whichever option happens to be selected first.

A daily budget sets roughly how much you want to spend per day, running indefinitely with no required end date. This suits ongoing, evergreen campaigns — the kind of always-on advertising a business runs continuously rather than for a specific limited window. It also tends to offer more day-to-day flexibility if you're actively managing spend and want to shift budget around based on performance as data comes in.

A lifetime budget sets a total amount you're willing to spend across a defined date range, and Meta's system decides how to pace that spend across the days in between — sometimes spending more on days it identifies as higher-opportunity, sometimes less, as long as the total stays within your set amount by the end date. This fits time-bound campaigns well: a festival sale, a product launch, an event promotion with a clear start and end. One detail worth knowing: ad scheduling — choosing specific hours or days for your ad to run, rather than running continuously — is a feature that currently only works with lifetime budgets, so if precise day-part control matters to your campaign, that's a relevant factor in the choice.

Budget TypeBest FitKey Trait
Daily BudgetOngoing, evergreen campaigns with no fixed end dateAverage spend per day; more flexible to adjust as you go
Lifetime BudgetTime-bound offers, launches, festival sales, eventsFixed total across a date range; unlocks ad scheduling by specific hours/days

For a genuine first-time advertiser without a hard deadline attached to the campaign — someone simply testing whether ads work for their business at all — a daily budget on an ongoing campaign is often the simpler, easier-to-manage starting choice, since it avoids needing to pick a precise date range before you have any performance data to base that decision on.

Audience Size and Creative Volume Affect What Budget Can Actually Do

Two factors that often get overlooked when setting a budget are how broad or narrow your target audience is, and how much ad creative (images, videos, ad copy variations) you're feeding into the campaign — both affect how efficiently a given budget actually gets used.

A very narrow audience — a small city neighborhood, an extremely specific interest combination — limits how many people Meta's system has to show your ad to, which can make it harder for even a reasonable budget to gather data quickly, since there simply aren't that many eligible people to reach each day. A very broad audience with no meaningful targeting, on the other hand, may spend efficiently in terms of volume but waste budget showing ads to people unlikely to ever become customers. The practical middle ground for most small businesses is a moderately defined audience — broad enough that Meta's delivery system has room to find the best-performing segments within it, specific enough that it's not wasting spend on clearly irrelevant people.

Creative volume matters for a related reason: a single ad, shown repeatedly to the same audience over weeks, tends to suffer from what's often called ad fatigue — the same people seeing it enough times that engagement drops and cost-per-result climbs, even if the underlying targeting and budget haven't changed. Having a small rotation of a few different creative variations (different images, different hooks, different formats) gives Meta's delivery system room to keep serving something relatively fresh, and also gives you comparative data on which creative approach actually resonates, rather than a single untested guess absorbing your entire budget.

Once a campaign has been running for a while, the next natural question is when it makes sense to spend more.

Signals that a campaign may be ready to scale generally include: consistently hitting a cost-per-result you're genuinely comfortable with, over a meaningful stretch of time rather than one unusually good day, along with enough total conversions that the pattern feels reliable rather than lucky. If a campaign has been reliably profitable at its current spend level for a couple of weeks, that's a more solid basis for increasing budget than a single strong day.

When you do decide to increase, doing it gradually tends to work better than large, sudden jumps. Meta's delivery algorithm has to essentially relearn optimal delivery patterns whenever a budget changes significantly, and a large sudden increase can temporarily disrupt performance while the system readjusts — sometimes described as resetting or disrupting the "learning phase." Smaller, incremental increases (a moderate percentage at a time, spaced out, rather than doubling overnight) tend to preserve performance more reliably than aggressive jumps.

Signals to pause and rethink instead of increasing spend include: cost-per-result climbing steadily over time with no clear explanation, very low or declining click-through rates suggesting the creative has become stale to your audience, or simply not having enough data yet to know either way. It's worth resisting the urge to respond to poor early performance by immediately spending more, on the assumption that more budget alone will fix an underlying problem with the offer, audience, or creative — often it doesn't, and it just means losing more money finding that out.

First-Time Advertiser Example Scenarios

These two scenarios are illustrative examples meant to show the reasoning in action — not templates to copy exactly, since your own numbers from Step 1 will differ.

Example: a local service business. Imagine a home-cleaning service in Lucknow where a typical customer is worth roughly ₹1,800 across their first few bookings, and the business can comfortably afford to spend around ₹350 to acquire a new customer while still being profitable. Rather than guessing a daily ad budget, the owner works backward: if roughly 1 in 8 leads from ads typically becomes a paying customer (a number they'd only really know from testing, but can start with a conservative estimate), each lead is worth acting on up to about ₹40–₹45 in acquisition cost. Setting a testing-phase daily budget that generates enough leads over two to three weeks to actually test that conversion assumption — rather than one so small it produces a handful of leads total — gives the business real data to work from rather than a guess extended indefinitely.

Example: a D2C e-commerce brand. Imagine a small skincare brand with an average order value of ₹950 and healthy margins, testing three different ad creatives to see which resonates. Rather than committing the full test budget to one creative, splitting a modest daily testing budget across all three (or letting Meta's system test them within one ad set) over a couple of weeks provides a clearer signal about which creative actually performs, before committing a larger scaling budget to the single best performer. Judging all three fairly requires giving each enough spend and time to gather real data — not declaring a winner after the first day based on whichever got a few more clicks.

Both examples are framed as illustrations of the reasoning process, not promises of what any specific business will experience — actual costs-per-lead, conversion rates, and results vary by industry, location, competition, and execution, and neither example should be read as a guaranteed outcome. The value of working through a scenario like this isn't the specific numbers, which will always be different for your business — it's practicing the habit of tying a budget decision back to your own margins and conversion assumptions, rather than picking a figure because it sounded reasonable or because a competitor was rumored to be spending it.

Common Budgeting Mistakes

Spending too little to gather meaningful data. A budget so small that it produces only a handful of clicks or impressions over a testing period doesn't give Meta's delivery system, or you, enough information to draw a real conclusion — it's neither a fair test of the offer nor of the platform.

Judging a campaign after only one or two days. Early performance, especially in the first day or two, often doesn't reflect how a campaign will settle once Meta's delivery system has had time to find the right audience within your targeting. Pulling the plug or dramatically changing course based on 24–48 hours of data is one of the most common reasons first-time advertisers conclude "ads don't work" when the campaign was never given a fair chance to show what it could do.

Increasing budget drastically overnight instead of gradually. As covered above, sudden large budget jumps can disrupt a campaign's delivery stability, sometimes making a previously well-performing campaign temporarily worse right after the increase — the opposite of the intended effect.

Not knowing your margin before setting a budget. Setting a budget without first understanding what a customer is actually worth, and what you can afford to spend acquiring one, means there's no real basis for judging whether the campaign's results are actually good for your business — you're comparing spend to a feeling rather than to a number.

Copying a competitor's assumed budget. It's tempting to base your budget on what you assume a competitor is spending, but you rarely have accurate visibility into their actual numbers, margins, or results — and even if you did, their business economics are their own, not necessarily transferable to yours.

Running a conversion-optimized campaign without proper tracking set up. As covered above, a campaign optimizing toward purchases or leads needs accurate tracking to actually learn and improve — without it, both you and Meta's delivery system are effectively working with incomplete information, no matter how well-reasoned the budget itself is.

A Simple Budget-Setting Checklist

Before opening Ads Manager, it's worth working through this in order:

  • Calculate your average order value or customer value, and your rough acceptable cost-per-result based on your margin
  • Set a testing-phase budget you're genuinely comfortable spending on learning, not just on immediate profit
  • Decide whether a daily or lifetime budget fits your campaign's timeline
  • Give the campaign enough time — generally at least a week to two weeks — before drawing conclusions from the data
  • Track cost-per-result specifically, not just impressions, reach, or clicks in isolation
  • Scale gradually in moderate increments once results are consistently solid, rather than making large jumps

What If You Can't Afford a Big Budget Right Now

It's worth being direct about this, because budget anxiety often stops small businesses from advertising at all. A small, honest testing budget you can genuinely afford to learn from is more valuable than not advertising at all, or than delaying indefinitely while waiting to feel ready with a bigger amount. Early on, the goal is learning what works for your specific business — which audience responds, which message lands, what your real numbers look like — not achieving significant scale immediately.

If your available budget is genuinely tight, it's more useful to narrow your targeting and creative focus (test one clear offer to one well-defined audience) than to spread a small amount thinly across many different audiences or ad variations at once, since a thinly spread small budget rarely generates enough data in any single direction to actually learn anything.

It's also worth separating "small budget" from "no strategy." Even a modest testing budget benefits from the same discipline as a larger one — knowing your numbers from Step 1, picking one clear objective, giving it enough time before judging results, and tracking cost-per-result rather than vanity metrics like impressions. A small, disciplined budget run this way tends to teach you more, faster, than a larger budget spent reactively without a clear framework behind it.

Testing, Retargeting, and Where Budget Should Shift Over Time

As a campaign matures beyond the initial testing phase, it's worth understanding that not all ad spend needs to be spent chasing entirely new audiences. Retargeting — showing ads specifically to people who've already interacted with your business, such as visiting your website or engaging with a previous ad or post — often converts at a meaningfully better rate than cold audience targeting, simply because those people already have some familiarity with your business.

Many advertisers find it useful to allocate the majority of testing budget toward reaching new audiences early on (since there isn't yet a retargeting audience built up), then gradually shift a portion of budget toward retargeting as enough website visitors or engaged users accumulate to make that audience meaningfully sized. This isn't a rigid rule, but a general pattern worth being aware of as your campaigns and audience data mature over the first few months. Retargeting audiences also decay over time if left unattended — someone who visited your site three months ago and never returned is a weaker retargeting prospect than someone who visited last week, so this is a shift that needs occasional revisiting rather than a one-time setup you configure and forget.

It's also worth noting that a landing page matters as much as the ad budget itself. A well-targeted ad sending traffic to a slow, confusing, or generic landing page will underperform regardless of how carefully the budget was planned — the ad's job is to earn the click, but the page it leads to has to actually convert that visitor, and no amount of budget optimization fixes a landing page that isn't doing its job.

A related, often-missed detail: for retargeting or conversion-optimized campaigns to work well, Meta needs a working pixel or conversions API setup on your website to actually track what happens after someone clicks the ad — a purchase, a form submission, a specific page view. Running a conversion-focused campaign without this tracking properly set up is a bit like driving with the odometer disconnected: the campaign can still run and spend budget, but neither you nor Meta's delivery system has accurate feedback on what's actually working, which undermines a lot of the budget-management advice above, since "track cost-per-result" only means something if the results are actually being tracked correctly in the first place.
