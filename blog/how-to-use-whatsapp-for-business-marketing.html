How to Use WhatsApp for Business Marketing

Practical, concrete ways to use WhatsApp Business features for marketing without feeling spammy.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 9 September 2026·19 min read

Key Takeaways

If you're already using WhatsApp to answer customer questions, take orders, or confirm bookings, you're closer to using it as a marketing channel than you might think — the gap is usually structure, not effort. Most businesses use WhatsApp reactively, replying to whoever messages first, without a deliberate system for catalogs, follow-ups, or staying in touch with people who've already shown interest. This article covers how to turn that informal usage into something closer to a proper channel, using the actual features available in the WhatsApp Business app, without turning your number into something customers eventually mute or block.

The short version: WhatsApp Business (the free app, distinct from the regular consumer WhatsApp) gives you a business profile, a product or service catalog, quick replies, automated greeting and away messages, labels for organizing conversations, and broadcast lists for reaching multiple opted-in contacts at once. Used with restraint and genuine value, these features let you run enquiries, follow-ups, and light promotional communication through a channel most of your customers already check dozens of times a day. Used carelessly — unsolicited bulk messages, no opt-in, excessive frequency — the same features get your number blocked and reported. This article covers both the setup and the judgment calls in between.

WhatsApp Business vs Regular WhatsApp: Start Here

WhatsApp Business is a separate, free app from Meta, built specifically for businesses and distinct from the standard consumer WhatsApp app. It's designed to run alongside or instead of a personal WhatsApp account, using your business phone number. The core features it adds that the regular app doesn't have include a business profile (with hours, address, website, and category), a product/service catalog, quick replies, automated greeting and away messages, labels for organizing chats, broadcast lists, and basic messaging statistics.

Based on currently available information about the app, the free version supports a catalog of up to 500 items and broadcast lists capped at 256 contacts per list, with the app itself usable on a handful of linked devices. For most small and mid-sized local businesses, this free tier covers the actual marketing needs described in this article. Larger businesses running high-volume automated messaging, multi-agent support teams, or CRM integrations typically graduate to the WhatsApp Business API, which is a separate, paid, developer-oriented product usually accessed through an official partner rather than downloaded as an app — that's covered briefly later in this piece, but the bulk of what's described here works with the free app alone.

It's worth checking current app documentation or your business's specific plan before assuming any particular feature or limit, since Meta updates these details periodically and availability can vary somewhat by region or account.

Setting Up a Product or Service Catalog

The catalog feature turns your WhatsApp business profile into a browsable showcase, letting customers see what you offer without you having to type out a list every time someone asks. Each catalog item can include a photo, name, price or price range, a short description, and in some cases a link back to your website for more detail.

Setting one up is straightforward: within the WhatsApp Business app, you add items one at a time through the business tools menu, uploading a clear photo and filling in the relevant details. The practical value shows up in daily use — instead of writing out "we have three types of facials, here are the prices" every time someone messages, you share the catalog or a specific item directly in the chat, and the conversation moves faster to the actual decision.

A few things make catalogs work better in practice. Photos matter enormously — a catalog with poor lighting or inconsistent framing undercuts trust even if the product itself is good, so it's worth investing a bit of effort in decent photography rather than using whatever phone snapshot is easiest. Keeping the catalog current matters too — items that are out of stock or discontinued but still listed create confusion and wasted back-and-forth. And clear pricing, where your business model allows it, reduces the number of "how much does this cost" messages you'd otherwise have to answer manually.

One limitation worth knowing: the native WhatsApp catalog doesn't include built-in checkout or live inventory syncing — it's a showcase that supports the conversation, not a full storefront. If a customer wants to buy something they see in the catalog, the actual transaction (payment, confirmation, delivery details) typically still happens through the conversation itself, a payment link, or a separate order process, unless a business has set up a more advanced API-based integration with an e-commerce platform.

This distinction matters when you're deciding how much to invest in the catalog versus other parts of your sales process. A well-built catalog reduces friction in the browsing and enquiry stage — customers can see what's on offer and roughly what it costs before committing to a longer conversation — but it doesn't remove the need for a human (or at least a well-set-up automated flow) to actually close the sale once interest is confirmed. Businesses that expect the catalog alone to generate sales without any follow-through on enquiries tend to be disappointed; the catalog's job is to make the enquiry stage faster and more informative, not to replace the conversation entirely.

It's also worth deciding, category by category, whether to list exact prices or a starting range. For straightforward, standardized products, exact pricing tends to build trust and reduce time spent answering "how much" messages. For services where the final price depends on specifics — a custom video shoot, a home renovation, a bespoke web development project — a clearly labeled starting range, with a note that a final quote depends on requirements, avoids both the vagueness of no pricing at all and the mismatch of a rigid number that doesn't reflect the actual variability in what you offer.

Using Broadcast Lists — Not Groups — for Updates

This distinction trips up a lot of businesses new to WhatsApp marketing. A broadcast list sends the same message individually to each contact on the list — recipients don't see each other, can't see who else received the message, and their replies come back to you privately rather than into a shared thread. A group, by contrast, is a shared conversation space where every member can see all messages and reply to everyone.

For business updates and light promotional messages, broadcast lists are almost always the better choice. Groups tend to feel intrusive for marketing purposes — customers rarely want to be in a visible group with strangers just because they bought from the same business, and groups also open the door to other members messaging each other or the business publicly, which can get messy fast.

The catch with broadcast lists on the free app is the 256-contact cap per list based on currently available information, along with a more important requirement: a broadcast message only reaches a contact if they already have your business number saved in their phone. This is a real practical constraint — it means broadcast lists work well for customers who've already interacted with you and saved your number, but they're not a tool for reaching people who haven't yet engaged with your business at all.

Quick Replies and Automated Greetings

Quick replies let you save commonly sent messages and pull them up instantly using a shortcut, rather than retyping the same answer to "what are your hours" or "do you deliver" multiple times a day. Based on currently available information, the free app supports a meaningful number of saved quick replies, retrieved by typing a forward slash in the message box.

Automated greeting messages send an immediate response to a new contact's first message, letting them know you've received it even if you can't reply right away — useful for setting expectations without requiring a live team member to be watching the chat constantly. Away messages do the equivalent outside business hours, so customers messaging late at night get an immediate, honest response about when they'll hear back rather than silence.

These features matter for perceived responsiveness more than they might seem to on paper. A customer who gets an immediate, even automated, acknowledgment is generally more patient waiting for a full human reply than one who sends a message into apparent silence and starts wondering if it was received at all.

Labels: Organizing Conversations So Nothing Gets Lost

Labels let you tag and filter conversations by status — new enquiry, quoted, booked, needs follow-up, completed, and so on, using whatever categories make sense for your business. This becomes genuinely useful once you're handling more than a handful of conversations a day, since it's easy to lose track of who you still owe a reply to or which enquiries went cold without a follow-up.

A practical labeling system for many small businesses might look like: "New Enquiry" for anything unanswered, "Follow Up" for conversations that need a nudge, "Booked/Confirmed" for closed sales, and "Past Customer" for people who've already completed a purchase and might be relevant for a future win-back or repeat-purchase message. Reviewing the "Follow Up" label regularly is one of the simplest ways to catch enquiries that would otherwise quietly go cold.

The discipline that makes labels useful isn't the labeling itself — it's the habit of actually reviewing each label on a set schedule, whether that's daily for "New Enquiry" or weekly for "Follow Up" and "Past Customer." Without that review habit, labels become a filing system nobody checks, which defeats the purpose. For a solo business owner, this might mean five minutes each morning scanning the "Follow Up" list before starting the day; for a small team, it might mean assigning one person responsibility for keeping that list at zero unanswered items.

Using WhatsApp Status for Soft Marketing

WhatsApp Status — the disappearing 24-hour update feature, similar in concept to Instagram Stories — is a genuinely underused tool for business marketing. Because it appears passively at the top of a contact's chat list rather than as an interruptive message, it functions as a low-pressure way to stay visible without the risk of feeling spammy that a direct message can carry.

Businesses use Status for behind-the-scenes content, new arrivals, limited-time offers, quick testimonials, or simple reminders — a salon might post a Status update on a quiet Tuesday afternoon, a restaurant might post today's special, a real estate agent might post a walkthrough clip of a new listing. Because Status doesn't require the same explicit opt-in scrutiny as a direct broadcast message (it's visible to any contact who has your number saved, similar to a social media story), it's a reasonable place to be a bit more frequent with lighter content than you would be in direct messages.

How to Market on WhatsApp Without Feeling Spammy

This is the concern most business owners have going in, and it's a legitimate one — a badly run WhatsApp marketing effort can genuinely damage a relationship with a customer in a way that a poorly performing social media ad usually can't, because WhatsApp feels personal in a way a public feed doesn't.

Get Explicit Opt-In Before Adding to Broadcast Lists

Never add a customer's number to a broadcast list simply because they messaged you once about an enquiry. A simple line — "would you like occasional updates on new arrivals/offers via WhatsApp?" — asked directly, gives the customer a real choice and sets the expectation that messages will follow. Building a list this way is slower than adding everyone by default, but it produces a far more receptive audience and dramatically reduces the risk of being blocked or reported.

Opt-in doesn't have to happen only through a direct question mid-conversation. A checkout or booking form that includes an explicit, unticked checkbox for WhatsApp updates, a small sign at a physical location inviting customers to opt in for offers, or a link shared after a purchase are all valid ways to build a list — the common thread across all of them is that the customer took a clear, affirmative action, rather than being added by default because their number was already in your phone. Keeping a record of how and when someone opted in is also useful practice, both for your own reference and in case a customer later asks why they're receiving messages from you.

Balance Promotional Messages With Genuinely Useful Ones

If every message from your business number is "buy now" or "sale ends today," customers will tune out or leave quickly. Mixing in messages that are useful independent of a purchase — a maintenance tip related to a product they bought, a reminder that's genuinely helpful to them, a piece of relevant information — keeps the channel feeling like a relationship rather than a constant sales pitch.

Respect Frequency

There's no universal number of messages per week that's "safe" — it depends heavily on your business type, what customers signed up expecting, and how valuable each message is. The practical signal to watch is engagement: if you notice replies dropping, more people leaving your broadcast lists (where visible), or an uptick in blocks, that's a direct signal you're messaging too often or the content isn't landing.

Never Use Purchased or Scraped Contact Lists

Messaging people who never gave you their number or never opted in is not a gray area — it's the single fastest way to get a business number restricted or banned, and it damages trust with anyone who does receive an unsolicited message. Every contact on a broadcast list should be someone who has interacted with your business directly and agreed, even informally, to receive updates.

Practical Customer Journeys Using WhatsApp

WhatsApp works best as a connective layer between other channels and an actual conversion, rather than as a standalone acquisition tool. A few common journeys illustrate this:

Instagram to WhatsApp to Booking

A potential customer sees a post or reel, clicks through to the business profile, and messages via a "message on WhatsApp" link rather than commenting publicly. From there, the conversation naturally moves to enquiry, catalog sharing, pricing, and — if it's a good fit — booking or purchase confirmation, all within the same thread rather than requiring the customer to navigate a separate booking system.

Website to WhatsApp to Conversion

A website visitor who's interested but not quite ready to fill out a formal contact form often responds better to a floating "chat on WhatsApp" button, since it feels lower-commitment than an email form and offers the possibility of an immediate reply. This is particularly effective for service businesses where pricing depends on specifics that are easier to clarify in conversation than through a static form.

Existing Customer to Reminder to Repeat Purchase

For businesses with a natural repurchase cycle — a salon, a subscription-adjacent product, a recurring service — a simple, well-timed reminder message ("it's been about six weeks since your last visit, would you like to book your next appointment?") converts noticeably better than hoping the customer remembers on their own, since most people simply don't track this themselves.

Writing Useful WhatsApp Messages

Good WhatsApp marketing messages tend to share a few characteristics regardless of business type. They're short enough to read in a few seconds on a phone screen. They're clear about what action you want the reader to take, whether that's replying, tapping a catalog link, or booking. They're timed to be relevant — a reminder sent near when someone would naturally need the service performs better than the same message sent at a random moment. And they sound like a person wrote them, not like an automated marketing blast, even when a template is being reused across many contacts.

A practical structure that works across most business types: state why you're reaching out, give the specific, relevant piece of information (an offer, a reminder, an update), and close with one clear next step rather than several competing calls to action. Avoid over-formatting with excessive emojis or capital letters, which tends to read as spam regardless of the actual content.

Timing deserves its own attention beyond just wording. A reminder message sent a full month before a service is typically due gets ignored and forgotten by the time it's relevant; the same message sent a few days before tends to land well. A promotional message sent very early in the morning or late at night, even if the content is fine, can register as intrusive simply because of when it arrived. Paying attention to when your specific audience tends to be active and receptive — which you can often infer from when they naturally message you — is a low-effort way to improve how messages land without changing the content at all.

It's also worth thinking about message length deliberately. WhatsApp is a conversational medium, and long paragraphs that would be perfectly normal in an email often feel overwhelming in a chat window. Breaking a longer update into two or three short messages, or using simple line breaks, tends to read more naturally than a single dense block of text.

Who Should Receive Which Message

Not every message belongs to every contact. New enquiries need direct, responsive answers to their specific questions rather than generic broadcast content. Existing customers benefit from reminders and light updates relevant to what they've already purchased. Lapsed customers — those who haven't engaged in a while — are better suited to an occasional, specific win-back message than to being included in every regular broadcast, since irrelevant frequent messages are exactly what drives disengagement in this group.

WhatsApp Business API for Growing Businesses

For businesses outgrowing the free app — high message volume, multiple team members needing shared access, or a need to integrate WhatsApp with a CRM or e-commerce platform — the WhatsApp Business API is the next tier. Unlike the free consumer-facing app, the API is typically accessed through an official Meta business partner or solution provider, involves per-message pricing for certain message categories, and removes several of the free app's limits, including the broadcast contact cap.

This is a meaningfully bigger commitment than the free app — it usually requires working with a third-party platform for setup, ongoing costs tied to message volume, and more technical integration work. For most small and growing local businesses, the free WhatsApp Business app comfortably covers the marketing and communication needs described in this article, and the API becomes relevant mainly once message volume, team size, or automation needs outgrow what a single device running the free app can reasonably manage.

Practical Campaign Ideas by Business Type

A few grounded examples of how different businesses put these features to use:

Restaurants and Event/Banquet Businesses

Sharing a catalog of menu packages for events, using Status to post daily specials, and sending a follow-up message a few days after a large order or booking to check satisfaction and gently invite a repeat booking.

Salons

Using labels to track clients due for a rebooking, sending a simple reminder near their typical cycle, and sharing before/after photos on Status as light, low-pressure marketing.

Real Estate

Sharing new listings directly with an opted-in broadcast list of active buyers, using quick replies for common questions about availability and pricing, and maintaining long-term contact with past clients for future referrals.

Local Service Businesses

Using automated away messages to manage expectations outside working hours, quick replies for common quote requests, and a simple catalog listing service packages and starting prices.

E-commerce and Product Businesses

Using the catalog as a lightweight secondary storefront for customers who prefer chatting to browsing a website, and broadcast updates for genuinely new arrivals rather than constant sales.

Professional Services

Using WhatsApp mainly for enquiry handling and follow-up rather than broadcast marketing, given the typically lower purchase frequency and higher trust requirements of this category.

Measuring Results

WhatsApp doesn't offer the same depth of analytics as a paid ad platform, but there's still enough to track meaningfully. Worth monitoring: the volume of enquiries coming in through WhatsApp specifically (tagging or labeling by source helps here), response rate and response time, how many enquiries convert to actual bookings or sales, and — for businesses using broadcast lists — how engagement (replies, click-throughs on shared links) trends over time as a proxy for whether your messaging frequency and content are landing well.

There's no universal benchmark for what a "good" enquiry-to-conversion rate looks like on WhatsApp, since this depends heavily on your industry, price point, and how well-qualified the enquiries are to begin with. The more useful practice is tracking your own numbers consistently over a few months and watching the trend, the same way you would for repeat purchase rate or any other retention metric.

A simple way to start, without any special tooling: keep a running count in a spreadsheet of new WhatsApp enquiries each week, how many were responded to within a reasonable window (say, a few hours during business time), and how many converted to a booking or sale. Over a few months this gives you a rough enquiry-to-conversion rate specific to your business, and — just as importantly — surfaces whether slow response time is costing you conversions, since response speed tends to correlate strongly with conversion rate on a channel as immediate as WhatsApp. If you notice conversion dropping while enquiry volume stays flat or rises, a slipping response time is one of the first things worth checking before assuming the messaging or offer itself is the problem.

Common Mistakes with WhatsApp Marketing

  • Adding contacts to broadcast lists or groups without consent. Assuming that because someone messaged once, they've agreed to receive ongoing updates — this is the fastest way to get blocked or reported.
  • Messaging too frequently. Treating WhatsApp like an unlimited-frequency channel because it's free, when in practice over-messaging damages the relationship faster here than on almost any other channel.
  • Never setting up a business profile or catalog. Operating from a personal-looking WhatsApp number with no business information, missing an easy, low-cost credibility boost.
  • Confusing groups with broadcast lists. Adding customers to a visible group for marketing purposes, which most customers find intrusive and which can lead to unwanted cross-talk between unrelated customers.
  • Using purchased or scraped contact lists. Messaging people with no prior relationship to the business, which is both ineffective and risks the number being restricted.
  • Sending only promotional content. Never mixing in genuinely useful, non-sales messages, which trains recipients to associate your number purely with sales pitches.
  • Ignoring labels and follow-up. Letting enquiries go unanswered or unlabeled, so warm leads quietly go cold without anyone noticing.

A Simple Setup Checklist

  • Download WhatsApp Business and complete the business profile fully — hours, address, category, website link.
  • Build a basic product/service catalog with clear photos and pricing where possible.
  • Set up an automated greeting message and an away message for outside business hours.
  • Create a small number of quick replies for your most common customer questions.
  • Set up a simple labeling system (new enquiry, follow-up, booked, past customer).
  • Only add customers to broadcast lists after asking for explicit opt-in.
  • Plan a light mix of promotional and genuinely useful messages before sending your first broadcast.

Bringing It Together

WhatsApp's real value as a marketing channel comes from the fact that it's already where your customers are paying attention, not from any single feature being uniquely powerful. A complete business profile, a clear catalog, sensible use of broadcast lists with real opt-in, and a habit of following up on enquiries will cover most of what a small or mid-sized business needs from this channel. The businesses that struggle with WhatsApp marketing are almost always the ones that treat it as a free mass-messaging tool rather than what it actually is — a direct, personal channel that rewards relevance and restraint and punishes anything that feels like spam. Used consistently, this kind of direct follow-up is one of the more effective ways to turn a one-time buyer into a repeat customer.
