How to Start a UGC Content Strategy for Your Brand

A clear, sequential starter process for launching a UGC content strategy.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 10 September 2026·19 min read

Knowing what UGC is doesn't tell you how to actually get it flowing for your own brand — and that gap is where most businesses get stuck. This article walks through a practical, sequential process: deciding between organic and hired UGC (or both), briefing creators properly, deciding what content to actually ask for, sorting out usage rights, and building a system you can repeat rather than a one-off project that fizzles out after the first batch of videos.

Before going further, it's worth being precise about terms, because "UGC" gets used loosely in ways that create confusion. Customer-generated content is content your actual customers make voluntarily or in response to a simple ask. Creator-generated UGC is content made by someone you hire specifically to produce authentic-feeling, UGC-style content, regardless of whether they're your customer. Influencer marketing is a different thing entirely — it relies on a creator's existing audience and personal credibility, not on the content's style. Testimonials are a specific content type (someone vouching for your product) that can appear within either organic or hired UGC. This article focuses on customer-generated and creator-generated UGC specifically, since those are the two practical paths a business can actually build a repeatable strategy around.

The Two Paths to UGC: Organic and Hired

Every UGC strategy runs through one, or both, of two tracks.

Organic UGC comes from real customers who create content about your product or service without being paid specifically to do so — a genuine review video, an unboxing post, a mention in a story. This content tends to carry a particularly high degree of trust precisely because it wasn't commissioned, but it's also less predictable and harder to direct.

Hired UGC comes from creators you pay specifically to produce content in the UGC style — casual, authentic-feeling, shot in a real setting rather than a studio — regardless of whether they've ever used your product before being briefed. This gives you far more control over timing, volume, and message, at the cost of the content not being a genuinely spontaneous customer experience.

Most brands that build a mature UGC content pipeline eventually use both tracks in parallel: organic UGC for authentic social proof and community-building, and hired UGC for a reliable, on-demand supply of ad-ready creative that doesn't depend on customers happening to post.

Path 1: Encouraging Organic Customer UGC

Getting real customers to create content starts with a simple, low-friction ask — most customers who'd be happy to share something never think to do it unless prompted. The ask tends to work best when it's specific and easy to act on: "tag us in your unboxing," "show us how you use this," rather than a vague "share your experience."

The point of first contact matters. Asking right after delivery, right after a positive support interaction, or right after a customer has clearly had a good experience tends to produce a much higher response rate than a generic, untargeted request sent to your entire list.

A small incentive can meaningfully increase participation without making the content feel purchased. Common approaches include a modest discount on a future order, entry into an occasional giveaway, or simply the offer of being featured on your brand's own page — many customers respond well to the visibility itself, especially smaller creators building their own following.

It helps to make sharing genuinely easy: a simple hashtag to use, a specific handle to tag, or a short, low-effort format suggestion (a 15-second clip is a much easier ask than "make us a video"). The lower the effort required, the more organic content you'll actually receive.

One honest limitation of organic UGC: you can encourage it, but you can't fully control its volume, quality, or timing. It's a genuine asset, but not something you can reliably schedule a content calendar around — which is exactly why most brands pair it with a hired-creator track.

Path 2: Hiring Dedicated UGC Creators

Hired UGC creators are people who specialize in producing authentic-feeling content on request, following a brief, typically without needing to have any prior history with your brand.

Creators can be found through a few common channels: dedicated UGC marketplaces and platforms built specifically to connect brands with creators, searching relevant content hashtags directly on Instagram or TikTok to find creators already making content in your niche and reaching out directly, and referrals from other brands or agencies who've worked with reliable creators before.

Arrangements with hired creators vary — some work per video, some offer content packages (a set number of videos for a flat rate), and some work on retainer for ongoing content needs. Rates depend heavily on the creator's experience, production quality, and how much usage the brand needs from the content, so there's no universal rate that applies evenly across creators or markets — this is something to discuss directly and confirm in writing before starting a project.

Choosing the Right Creators: What Actually Matters

It's tempting to sort potential creators by follower count, but for genuine UGC work, follower count is often close to irrelevant — the content isn't being distributed through the creator's own audience, so their reach matters far less than it would for an influencer partnership.

What actually predicts whether a creator will produce usable, effective content:

  • Audience fit — does their existing content style and tone match the audience you're actually trying to reach, even if their personal following is small?
  • Communication — do they respond promptly, ask clarifying questions, and understand a brief without excessive back-and-forth?
  • On-camera comfort — can they speak naturally to camera without sounding scripted or stiff, since forced delivery is one of the fastest ways UGC content starts to feel like an obvious ad?
  • Content quality — is their existing portfolio well-lit, reasonably framed, and technically usable, even within an intentionally casual style?
  • Niche relevance — do they have any genuine familiarity with your product category, which tends to produce more credible-sounding delivery than someone entirely unfamiliar with it?
  • Authenticity — does their delivery feel like a real recommendation, or does it read as clearly performed?
  • Reliability — do they deliver on time and follow through on agreed revisions, which matters enormously once you're relying on a steady content pipeline?

A creator with a modest personal following who consistently delivers authentic, well-shot, on-brief content is frequently more valuable to a UGC strategy than a creator with a large following who struggles to sound natural on camera. This is one of the more counterintuitive but important lessons for brands new to UGC.

Writing a Brief Creators Can Actually Use

A weak brief is probably the single most common reason hired UGC content comes back unusable or off-brand. A strong brief gives a creator enough direction to stay on message, without over-scripting them to the point that the content loses its natural, authentic feel.

A practical UGC creator brief should cover:

  • Objective — what is this content actually meant to achieve (awareness, trust-building, direct conversion)?
  • Audience — who is this content speaking to, in plain terms?
  • Key message — the one or two things that genuinely need to come across
  • Product or service details — what it is, how it's used, and any details the creator needs to represent it accurately
  • Required talking points — specific things that must be mentioned (pricing, a key feature, a claim you're comfortable making)
  • Prohibited claims — anything the creator should explicitly avoid saying, especially around health, efficacy, or guarantees
  • Hook ideas — a few example opening lines or moments to spark the creator's own approach, not a rigid script to read verbatim
  • Call to action — what should the viewer do after watching?
  • Visual requirements — orientation (usually vertical for most platforms), lighting expectations, and any specific shots needed (product close-ups, packaging, etc.)
  • Duration — target length, since this affects pacing and what can realistically be covered
  • Format — talking-head, demo, unboxing, testimonial, or another specific structure
  • Deadline — a clear delivery date
  • Delivery format — file type, resolution, and how the content should be sent
  • Revision expectations — how many rounds of revision are included, and what counts as a reasonable revision request
  • Usage rights — where and for how long the brand can use the finished content (covered in more detail below)

The most useful briefs give creators talking points, not a script. Handing someone a word-for-word script to read almost always produces stiff, obviously-performed delivery — the opposite of what UGC is supposed to feel like. Giving clear points to hit, paired with example tone and a couple of reference videos in a similar style, tends to produce far more natural, usable content.

Deciding What Content to Actually Ask For

Different UGC formats serve different points in a customer's decision process, and matching the format to the goal is a large part of building an effective strategy rather than just accumulating content.

Hooks and problem/solution structure

Short, attention-grabbing openers that name a specific problem before introducing the product as a solution tend to work well for cold-audience awareness content, where the goal is stopping the scroll before making any pitch.

Demonstrations

Showing the product actually being used works well when the product's value is easier to show than to explain — a tool, a beauty product application, an app's interface in use.

Testimonials and reviews

Direct statements of satisfaction or results build trust, and tend to work especially well further down the funnel, when a viewer is already considering the product and looking for reassurance rather than initial awareness.

Tutorials and how-to content

Educational, instructional content tends to serve both awareness (useful content that happens to feature the product) and conversion (showing exactly how to get value from a purchase), and often performs well organically because it's genuinely useful independent of the sale.

Unboxing content

Particularly effective for physical products, unboxing content plays into genuine anticipation and works well for both organic sharing and paid awareness content.

Before/after content

Where genuinely appropriate to the product category (and where claims can be made honestly and without misleading framing), before/after formats are persuasive for results-driven products — though this format needs particular care around accuracy and any relevant advertising guidelines.

Lifestyle and product-in-use content

Showing the product woven naturally into everyday life, rather than isolated and highlighted, tends to work well for retargeting audiences who are already familiar with the brand and need a final nudge rather than an introduction.

A practical rule of thumb: match the format to the funnel stage. Unboxing and hook-driven content for cold awareness audiences, testimonials and demos for consideration-stage audiences, and lifestyle or urgency-driven content for retargeting audiences close to a decision.

Getting Usage Rights Sorted

This is one of the most commonly skipped steps in UGC production, and it can create real problems later if it's overlooked. Before publishing hired UGC content — especially if it will run as paid advertising — it's important to clarify, in writing, exactly what the brand is permitted to do with it.

A usage rights agreement should specify:

  • Which platforms the content can be used on (organic social, paid ads, website, email, etc.)
  • How long the brand has the right to use the content (a fixed term, or in perpetuity)
  • Whether the content can be edited, repurposed, or combined with other footage
  • Whether the creator retains any rights to reuse or reference the content themselves
  • What compensation, if any, applies to extended or expanded usage beyond what was originally agreed

For organic customer UGC, this is simpler but still worth doing properly — a clear, upfront statement when asking for content (something like "by tagging us, you agree we may repost this") covers the basics, though for any content being used in paid advertising specifically, getting explicit permission directly from the customer is the safer approach.

Testing and Scaling What Works

UGC content, especially the hired-creator kind, benefits enormously from a test-and-scale approach rather than committing heavily to one style or creator upfront.

A practical approach: commission a small initial batch — a handful of pieces from a few different creators, in a few different formats — and run them as ads to a modest budget before deciding what to scale. This reveals which creators, hooks, and formats are actually resonating with your specific audience, rather than guessing based on assumptions.

Once a pattern emerges — a particular creator's delivery style performing consistently well, or a specific format (say, demonstration content) outperforming others — that's the signal to commission more content in that direction, rather than spreading budget evenly across everything that was tested.

It's worth tracking performance at the individual creative level, not just campaign-level results, since UGC performance can vary significantly between individual pieces of content even within the same format and creator.

UGC for Different Kinds of Businesses

UGC works differently depending on the business model, and it's worth thinking through how it applies to your specific category rather than copying a generic template.

D2C, fashion, and beauty brands tend to have the most natural fit for UGC, since products are visual, easy to demonstrate, and often purchased partly on the strength of how they look or perform in real use.

Restaurants can use UGC effectively through customer-shot content of dishes, the dining experience, or genuine reactions — often sourced organically rather than through hired creators, given the local nature of the business.

Apps and SaaS businesses can use UGC-style content to show real usage, walk through a specific use case, or share a genuine outcome, which tends to feel more credible than polished feature-list marketing for this category.

Local businesses — salons, gyms, clinics, services — often do best with organic UGC from real local customers, since hyper-local authenticity tends to matter more than production polish for this audience.

Education businesses can use UGC from students or learners describing their genuine experience or outcome, which tends to carry more weight than institutional marketing copy.

Service businesses more broadly can use client testimonial-style UGC, though this often requires more deliberate sourcing since service outcomes are less visually obvious than a physical product.

Real estate can use UGC-style content from buyers or tenants sharing their genuine experience with a property or agent, which works well as trust-building content alongside more traditional property footage.

Where UGC Actually Gets Used

UGC isn't limited to organic social posting — its most valuable applications are often in paid contexts, where the authentic style tends to outperform polished traditional ad creative on cost and engagement.

  • Organic social — feed posts, stories, and reels featuring customer or creator content
  • Meta Ads — UGC-style creative frequently performs well as ad content specifically because it doesn't look like a traditional ad in-feed
  • Landing pages and product pages — embedding UGC video near a purchase decision point can meaningfully support conversion
  • Retargeting — UGC testimonials and demonstrations work well for audiences who already know the brand and need reassurance
  • Awareness campaigns — hook-driven UGC content is often used specifically to interrupt the scroll for cold audiences

Avoiding the "Obvious Advertisement" Trap

The entire value of UGC depends on it not feeling like a traditional ad — the moment it does, much of its credibility advantage disappears. A few practical habits help avoid this:

  • Give creators talking points rather than a rigid script, so delivery sounds natural rather than recited
  • Avoid stacking too many required talking points into one piece of content — a video trying to cover five separate messages usually ends up sounding like a list of ad copy read aloud
  • Let creators shoot in their own natural setting and style rather than forcing a specific visual template across every creator
  • Review content for phrases that sound like marketing copy rather than something a real person would actually say, and flag those for a natural rewrite in revisions

Common Mistakes When Starting UGC

Choosing creators by follower count alone. As covered above, this is one of the most common and costly mistakes, since follower count has little bearing on whether hired content will actually feel authentic or perform well.

Writing generic, one-size-fits-all scripts. Over-scripted content tends to sound performed rather than genuine, undermining the entire purpose of the UGC format.

Overloading a single piece of content with too many talking points. This produces content that feels like a list of features rather than a natural recommendation.

Sending vague or incomplete briefs. Poor briefs are the most direct cause of unusable or off-brand content — creators can only deliver what they clearly understand is expected.

Weak or missing hooks. Content that doesn't grab attention in the first couple of seconds rarely gets watched long enough to deliver its message, regardless of how good the rest of the video is.

Unrealistic expectations about turnaround or quality at very low budgets. Rushed briefs or unreasonably tight timelines tend to produce rushed, lower-quality content.

Skipping usage rights clarity. This can create real problems later, especially if content ends up being used in paid advertising without clear, documented permission.

Not testing before scaling. Committing heavily to one creator or format without first testing a smaller batch means missing the chance to learn what actually resonates before spending significantly.

Not measuring results at all. Without tracking performance, it's impossible to know which creators, formats, or hooks are actually worth investing further in.

Making every piece of content look identical. Over-standardizing UGC (forcing every creator into the same visual template, same hook structure, same pacing) undermines the natural variation that makes UGC feel authentic in the first place.

Measuring UGC Performance

What "success" looks like for a piece of UGC content depends entirely on the campaign objective, so it's worth being specific about what you're actually trying to measure rather than applying one blanket metric to everything.

Useful metrics to track, depending on the goal, include:

  • Hook and early retention — how many viewers watch past the first few seconds, which reflects how well the opening actually grabs attention
  • Watch time and completion rate — how much of the video people actually watch through
  • Engagement — likes, comments, shares, and saves for organic content
  • Click-through rate — for paid content driving toward a landing page or product page
  • Landing page behavior — whether traffic from a specific piece of content behaves differently once it lands (time on page, scroll depth)
  • Leads or purchases — the ultimate conversion metric for content tied directly to a sales goal
  • Cost per result — for paid UGC creative, how efficiently a specific piece of content is driving the desired outcome relative to spend
  • Creative-level performance — comparing individual pieces of content against each other, rather than only looking at overall campaign averages

It's worth resisting the temptation to compare your results against generic industry benchmarks for UGC performance, since actual numbers vary enormously by industry, audience, platform, and campaign objective. What matters more is tracking your own performance consistently over time and using that as the baseline for deciding what to scale.

The Full UGC Workflow, Start to Finish

Bringing everything together, a repeatable UGC production process typically follows this sequence:

  1. Goal — define what this content needs to achieve
  2. Audience — clarify who it's speaking to
  3. Format — choose the content type that matches the goal and funnel stage
  4. Creator — select based on fit and reliability, not follower count
  5. Brief — write clear talking points, not a rigid script
  6. Product — brief the creator thoroughly on what they're representing
  7. Shoot — the creator produces the content
  8. Submission — content is delivered for review
  9. Review — check against the brief and brand guidelines
  10. Editing — light editing for pacing, captions, or trimming as needed
  11. Approval — final sign-off before publishing
  12. Publishing — content goes live, organically or as an ad
  13. Testing — run a small batch to see what performs
  14. Optimization — double down on what's working, refine or drop what isn't

Treating this as a repeatable pipeline, rather than a one-time project, is what separates businesses that get lasting value from UGC from those that produce one batch of content and stop.

A Simple UGC Starter Checklist

  • Decide whether you're pursuing organic UGC, hired creators, or both
  • Write a clear, specific brief with talking points rather than a rigid script
  • Select creators based on audience fit and reliability, not just follower count
  • Get usage rights confirmed in writing before publishing hired content as ads
  • Test a small batch of content before committing to a larger production run
  • Track performance at the individual content level to inform what to scale
  • Build UGC into an ongoing pipeline rather than treating it as a one-time project

Setting Realistic Expectations for What UGC Can and Can't Do

It's worth being upfront about what UGC is genuinely good for, and where expectations sometimes run ahead of what the format can realistically deliver, since misaligned expectations are a common reason businesses abandon UGC after a disappointing first attempt.

UGC tends to excel at building trust quickly, especially for products or services where potential customers are naturally skeptical of traditional advertising. It performs well as scroll-stopping content in paid social feeds specifically because it doesn't look like an ad at first glance. It's also generally faster and less expensive to produce per piece than traditional studio advertising, which makes it practical to test multiple angles, hooks, and creators without committing to one expensive production.

What UGC doesn't automatically do is guarantee performance. A piece of UGC content with a weak hook, an unclear message, or a mismatched creator can underperform just as easily as any other kind of content — the "authentic" format doesn't exempt it from needing a genuinely good idea behind it. UGC also isn't a replacement for having a real product or service that holds up; if the underlying offer has problems, authentic-feeling content will surface honest reactions to those problems just as readily as it surfaces praise for what's working.

It's also not something that scales instantly. Building a reliable pipeline of creators, briefs, and tested formats takes a few rounds of iteration before a brand typically finds its footing — treating the first batch of content as a learning exercise rather than a final judgment on whether UGC "works" for the business tends to produce better long-term results.

Platform Considerations for UGC in 2026

UGC-style content today primarily runs across Instagram Reels, Facebook, and short-form video placements within Meta's advertising network, alongside organic posting on the same platforms. Vertical, short-form video remains the dominant format for UGC because it matches how the format is actually consumed — quickly, on mobile, often muted with captions on. Since platform features and ad formats continue to evolve, it's worth checking current specifications (aspect ratio, length limits, caption requirements) directly on the platform you're planning to use before finalizing a creator brief, rather than assuming last year's specs still apply.

Captions deserve specific attention, since a meaningful share of video is watched without sound, at least initially. Briefs for hired creators should account for this — either by requesting on-screen text callouts of the key message, or by planning to add captions in the light editing pass after content is delivered.

Avoiding Common Sourcing Pitfalls

Beyond creator selection itself, a few practical sourcing mistakes are worth watching for specifically.

Rushing the vetting process. Watching a creator's existing content closely — not just skimming their profile — before committing to a paid arrangement helps avoid mismatches in tone, quality, or reliability that only become obvious after a brief has already been paid for.

Working with only one creator. Relying on a single creator for an entire content strategy creates both a bottleneck (if they're unavailable or slow to deliver) and a blind spot (you never learn whether a different style or delivery might perform better). Testing across a small handful of creators, even early on, tends to surface useful insight faster.

Not building a real relationship with reliable creators. Once a creator proves reliable and produces content that performs well, treating that relationship as ongoing — clear communication, fair and prompt payment, constructive feedback — tends to produce better results over time than treating every project as a one-off transaction with a new creator.

Ignoring creator feedback on the brief. Experienced UGC creators often have a good instinct for what will and won't feel natural on camera. A creator pushing back on an overly scripted talking point, or suggesting a more natural way to phrase something, is usually worth listening to rather than insisting on the original wording.

Building a Lasting UGC Engine

The businesses that get consistent, compounding value from UGC are the ones that treat it as an ongoing system rather than a single campaign. That means maintaining relationships with a small group of reliable creators, continuously testing new hooks and formats, feeding organic customer content back into the strategy as it comes in, and steadily building a library of authentic content that can be reused, repurposed, and refreshed across paid and organic channels over time.

Starting is genuinely simpler than it might seem: pick one path (organic, hired, or both), write one clear brief, produce a small first batch, and see what actually resonates with your specific audience before scaling further. The framework above gives you the structure — the results come from actually running it.
