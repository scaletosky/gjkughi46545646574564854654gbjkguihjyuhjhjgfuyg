5 Signs Your Website Urgently Needs a Redesign

Clear, objective signs to check against their own site, giving them confidence in the decision either way.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 9 September 2026·18 min read

Quick Answer

Before going through each sign in depth, here's the full list so you can see where you'll likely land: If none of these apply, your website's underlying structure is probably fine, even if it could use small refinements. If two or more clearly apply, a redesign is worth seriously considering.

Key Takeaways
  • Redesigning purely for a fresher look without addressing the underlying speed or mobile issues that were actually driving visitors away — the site ends up looking nicer but performing just as poorly.
  • Leaving outdated content in place through a redesign, effectively carrying old, misleading information into a shiny new wrapper.
  • Assuming a full redesign is needed when a handful of targeted fixes would have solved the actual problem for a fraction of the cost and time.
  • Skipping the audit step entirely and jumping straight to "let's redesign the whole thing" based on a vague feeling rather than specific, testable signs.
  • Not planning for SEO continuity during a redesign, leading to a real (and often significant) drop in search visibility right after launch due to broken URLs or lost content.

If you're wondering whether your website is actually hurting your business, or just looks a little dated, there's a practical way to find out: check it against a handful of specific, testable signs rather than going on a gut feeling. Some of those signs point toward a genuine redesign. Others point toward smaller, targeted fixes that solve the real problem without a full rebuild.

This guide walks through five concrete signs, how to actually test for each one on your own site in a few minutes, and — just as importantly — how to tell whether what you're facing needs a complete redesign or something far less drastic. By the end, you should be able to look at your own website and know, with some confidence, which category it falls into.

Quick Self-Check: Do Any of These 5 Signs Apply to You?

Before going through each sign in depth, here's the full list so you can see where you'll likely land:

  • Sign 1: It's not mobile-friendly
  • Sign 2: It loads slowly
  • Sign 3: Visitors can't find what they need quickly
  • Sign 4: The design looks dated compared to competitors
  • Sign 5: It doesn't reflect what you actually offer now

If none of these apply, your website's underlying structure is probably fine, even if it could use small refinements. If two or more clearly apply, a redesign is worth seriously considering. If only one applies, it often points toward a smaller, targeted fix rather than starting over completely — the sections after the five signs walk through how to tell the difference.

Sign 1: It's Not Mobile-Friendly

This is usually the most consequential sign on this list, because for most businesses, the majority of website visitors are now arriving on a phone, not a desktop or laptop.

How to test it yourself: open your own website on your actual phone — not by resizing a browser window on your computer, which doesn't replicate how mobile rendering, touch targets, and load behaviour actually work. Look specifically for: text that's too small to read without zooming, buttons or links placed too close together to tap accurately, horizontal scrolling that shouldn't be there, images that overflow the screen width, or a navigation menu that's awkward or broken on a small screen.

Worth knowing: Google retired its standalone Mobile-Friendly Test tool in December 2023, and now points site owners toward PageSpeed Insights instead, which covers mobile usability as part of its broader report alongside speed and Core Web Vitals. Running your site through PageSpeed Insights (pagespeed.web.dev) and checking the mobile tab will surface layout and usability issues alongside performance data in one place.

Why it matters beyond appearance: a poor mobile experience doesn't just look unpolished — it actively costs you visitors and, separately, can affect how your site performs in Google search results, since Google evaluates mobile usability as part of its overall page experience signals. A visitor who has to pinch-zoom to read your services or struggles to tap your phone number is a visitor who's likely to leave rather than persist.

What can be fixed without a full redesign: if the underlying site is built on a reasonably modern, responsive framework but has a few specific mobile issues — an oversized image, a menu that doesn't collapse properly, text that's slightly too small — these are often fixable with targeted CSS and layout adjustments rather than rebuilding the entire site.

When it points toward a redesign: if the site was built years ago on a fixed-width, desktop-only layout with no responsive framework underneath it at all, mobile-friendliness usually can't be patched in — the structural foundation itself needs to be rebuilt to be responsive from the ground up.

Sign 2: It Loads Slowly

How to test it yourself: run your website's key pages — homepage, main service or product pages, contact page — through a free tool like Google PageSpeed Insights. Look at both the Lab Data (a simulated test) and, if available, the Field Data (real user data collected from actual visitors via Chrome), since these can tell somewhat different stories. Pay particular attention to the Core Web Vitals metrics: Largest Contentful Paint (how quickly the main content becomes visible), and Cumulative Layout Shift (how much the page jumps around as it loads) — these are among the metrics Google's own systems associate with page experience quality.

Why it matters: slow-loading pages are consistently associated with higher bounce rates — visitors leaving before the page even finishes loading — and slow load speed can also work against you in search rankings, since Google factors page experience signals into how pages are ranked, particularly on mobile. A slow site isn't just an inconvenience; it's actively losing you visitors before they ever see what you have to offer.

Common causes worth checking first: unoptimised, oversized images (often the single biggest culprit), too many third-party scripts or tracking pixels loaded on every page, an overloaded or cheap hosting plan, and outdated plugins or page builders that add unnecessary code weight.

What can be fixed without a full redesign: image compression and modern formats, removing unused scripts and plugins, enabling caching, and — where the platform allows — upgrading hosting. These are genuinely often enough to move a slow site into acceptable territory without touching the design at all.

When it points toward a redesign: if the site is built on an old, heavy platform or theme with bloated code baked into its core structure, or if it's been patched together with years of accumulated plugins and workarounds, performance fixes alone tend to hit a ceiling — a rebuild on a leaner, modern foundation becomes the more reliable path to genuinely fast load times.

Sign 3: Visitors Can't Find What They Need Quickly

How to test it yourself: hand your phone or laptop to someone unfamiliar with your business — a friend, a family member, someone who's never seen your site — and ask them to find your contact information, or your main service or product, within about 10 seconds, without any hints from you. Watch where they hesitate, where they click by mistake, and where they give up.

This test tends to be genuinely revealing, because business owners are the worst-placed people to judge their own site's navigation — you already know where everything is, so confusing structure that would trip up a real visitor often reads as perfectly obvious to you.

Why it matters: confusing navigation doesn't just frustrate visitors — it directly costs conversions. Someone who can't quickly find your phone number, your pricing, or the specific service they were searching for simply leaves and finds a competitor whose site made it easier. Every extra click or moment of confusion between a visitor's intent and the action you want them to take is an opportunity for them to abandon the process.

What can be fixed without a full redesign: often, navigation problems come down to menu structure, labeling, and the absence of clear calls-to-action — these can frequently be reorganised without touching the site's overall visual design or underlying code. Renaming a confusing menu label, adding a persistent "Contact" or "Book Now" button, or reordering menu items by actual visitor priority can meaningfully improve this without a rebuild.

When it points toward a redesign: if the site's information architecture is fundamentally tangled — services buried three menu levels deep, no consistent navigation pattern across pages, or a structure that grew organically over years without any real planning — reorganising within the existing structure often just shuffles the confusion around rather than solving it. That's usually a sign the site needs to be rethought from the ground up.

Sign 4: The Design Looks Dated Compared to Competitors

How to test it yourself: open your website side-by-side with two or three direct competitors' sites, ideally ones you know are doing well. Look at this as objectively as you can — outdated visual trends (certain gradient styles, dense clip-art-style icons, cramped layouts, small default fonts), inconsistent spacing, and colour or imagery choices that feel visibly older than what competitors are using.

Design trends shift gradually but noticeably over a few years — what looked current in 2019 or 2020 often reads as dated by 2026, in the same way clothing or interior design trends age. This isn't about chasing every micro-trend, but a website that's visibly behind current norms can quietly undermine trust before a visitor even reads your content.

Why it matters: like it or not, visual design functions as a trust signal. A visitor forming a quick impression of your business's professionalism and credibility is, often unconsciously, using your website's design quality as a proxy for how seriously to take the business itself — rightly or wrongly. This effect tends to be strongest in categories where trust matters heavily before any transaction, like professional services, real estate, or healthcare.

What can be fixed without a full redesign: sometimes a visual refresh — updated colours, fonts, imagery, and spacing applied within the existing site structure — is enough to modernise the feel without touching the underlying code or layout logic. This is a meaningfully smaller project than a full redesign and can often be done relatively quickly.

When it points toward a redesign: if the dated feel is baked into the layout itself — an outdated grid structure, a homepage format that no longer matches how modern sites present information, or design constraints imposed by an old page-builder template — a surface-level refresh won't fully solve it, and a more complete redesign becomes the more effective path.

Sign 5: It Doesn't Reflect What You Actually Offer Now

How to test it yourself: go through your website page by page and compare it against what your business genuinely offers, charges, and does today. Look for discontinued services still listed, outdated pricing, old team photos or bios for people no longer with the business, old testimonials or case studies that no longer represent your current work, and outdated contact details or business hours.

Businesses evolve — services get added and dropped, pricing changes, teams change — but websites often don't keep pace, quietly drifting out of sync with reality over months or years without anyone deciding to actively neglect it.

Why it matters: this is one of the more damaging signs on this list, because it doesn't just look unprofessional — it can actively mislead visitors and create real friction. Someone who calls expecting a price they saw online, only to be quoted something different, starts the relationship on the wrong foot. A service listed that you no longer offer wastes both the visitor's time and yours when they enquire about it.

What can be fixed without a full redesign: in most cases, this is purely a content problem, not a design or technical one. Updating text, pricing, team information, and testimonials can usually be done directly within the existing site structure, especially if it's built on a content management system that allows straightforward editing.

When it points toward a redesign: if updating the content is difficult because the site's structure makes editing genuinely cumbersome — no proper content management system, requiring a developer for every small text change, or a rigid template that can't accommodate new services or sections — that difficulty itself becomes a legitimate reason to redesign, since an unmaintainable site will keep drifting out of date regardless of good intentions.

How These Signs Show Up Differently Across Business Types

The five signs above apply broadly, but how urgently each one matters — and how it's best fixed — genuinely varies depending on the kind of business and how customers actually reach it.

Local business (salon, gym, clinic): Sign 1 (mobile-friendliness) tends to be the most urgent here, since the overwhelming majority of local searches — "salon near me," "gym in [area]" — happen on a phone, often while someone is already out and about deciding where to go right now. A slow or confusing mobile experience at that exact moment usually just sends the visitor straight to the next search result. Sign 5 also matters more than it might seem, since local businesses tend to update prices, timings, and offers frequently, and a site quoting last year's rates creates an awkward moment at the front desk.

Restaurant: Sign 3 (navigation) is often the quiet problem here — a menu buried behind multiple clicks, or a PDF menu that's slow to load on mobile data, directly costs bookings and orders. Sign 5 shows up as outdated menu items, old operating hours (especially around festivals or holidays, which shift every year), or a discontinued delivery partnership still listed.

Real estate: Sign 4 (dated design) carries unusually heavy weight in this category, because a website is often a genuine proxy for how seriously a buyer or investor takes the developer or agent, given the size of the financial decision involved. Sign 2 (speed) also matters more here than average, since real estate sites tend to be image-heavy — property photos, floor plans, brochures — and unoptimised versions of these assets can make an otherwise strong listing feel sluggish and untrustworthy.

D2C brand: Sign 2 (speed) has an unusually direct line to revenue in e-commerce, where even small increases in load time are consistently associated with measurable drops in conversion and completed checkouts. Sign 3 (navigation) also matters significantly — a visitor unable to quickly find the right product size, variant, or category simply abandons the browsing session rather than searching further.

Professional service (accountant, consultant, lawyer): Sign 4 (dated design) and Sign 5 (outdated content) both carry more weight than usual, since these businesses sell trust and expertise before any other value, and a visitor forming a first impression from an outdated-looking site with an old team roster is quietly discounting the business's credibility before ever making contact.

Redesign, Refresh, or Just a Fix? Understanding the Difference

One of the most common and costly mistakes is treating every website problem as requiring a full redesign, when several genuinely different levels of intervention exist, each suited to different situations:

Type of WorkWhat It InvolvesBest Suited For
Content updateUpdating text, pricing, images, and information within the existing siteSign 5 issues, and general upkeep — no structural or visual problems
Technical/performance optimisationImage compression, caching, hosting upgrades, code cleanupSign 2 issues, where the underlying platform is reasonably modern
Visual refreshUpdated colours, fonts, imagery, and spacing within the existing layout structureSign 4 issues, where the layout itself is sound but feels visually dated
Conversion optimisationImproving calls-to-action, forms, and page flow to convert more visitors without changing the overall lookSites that look fine and get traffic but convert poorly
Full redesignRebuilding the site's structure, layout, and often underlying code from the ground upMultiple signs applying at once, or a structural limitation blocking smaller fixes

The practical takeaway: a website can have a visual problem without needing a complete rebuild, and equally, a website that looks acceptable on the surface can still have deeper UX, performance, or conversion issues that a visual refresh alone won't touch. Diagnosing which category you're actually in — using the five signs above — matters more than defaulting straight to "redesign" because that's the most commonly discussed option.

Factors That Tip the Decision Toward a Full Redesign

Beyond the five signs themselves, a handful of broader factors are worth weighing when deciding between fixing the existing site and starting fresh:

  • Age and platform. A site built more than five or six years ago, especially on a now-outdated platform or heavily customised old template, often has structural limitations that make ongoing patching increasingly inefficient compared to rebuilding.
  • Code quality and technical debt. A site that's been modified repeatedly over years by different people, with no consistent underlying structure, can become genuinely harder and more expensive to maintain than starting over.
  • Security and ongoing maintenance. Old, unpatched platforms and plugins carry real security risk, and if the platform itself is no longer actively maintained or supported, that risk only grows over time.
  • Changed business goals. If the website was built for a business that looked meaningfully different from what it is today — different services, different target customer, different scale — the fundamental structure may no longer fit, regardless of individual page fixes.
  • Future requirements. If you're planning to add functionality the current platform genuinely can't support — online booking, e-commerce, a members' area — that's a legitimate reason to rebuild on a more capable foundation now rather than working around limitations indefinitely.
  • SEO foundations. A site with fundamentally poor technical SEO structure — broken heading hierarchy, missing meta data across the board, poor URL structure — can sometimes be improved incrementally, but a redesign is a natural opportunity to fix these systematically rather than patching around old mistakes.

A Simple Self-Audit Checklist

Work through this in order — it mirrors the five signs above and takes most business owners well under an hour:

  • Open the site on your actual phone and try to complete the main action you want visitors to take
  • Run your key pages through Google PageSpeed Insights and note the mobile score and any flagged issues
  • Hand the site to someone unfamiliar with your business and time how long it takes them to find your contact info or main offering
  • Compare your homepage side-by-side with two or three competitors, as objectively as possible
  • Go through every page and confirm services, pricing, team details, and contact information are current
  • Note how many of the five signs genuinely apply, and how difficult each one would be to fix within the current site

If you come out of this audit with one applicable sign and it's something like outdated pricing or a slow image, you likely have a straightforward fix on your hands. If you come out with three or more signs applying, or a fix that keeps running into structural limitations, that's a reasonably strong signal a redesign is the more sensible path forward.

It's worth repeating the audit periodically even after you've decided the site is fine for now — these aren't one-time checks. Design norms shift, competitors update their sites, content quietly goes stale, and hosting or platform conditions change. Many businesses find that revisiting this same five-point check every six to twelve months catches small drifts early, before they accumulate into the kind of multi-sign situation that eventually forces an urgent, more expensive redesign under time pressure rather than a planned one on your own schedule.

What a Redesign Actually Involves

If the signs above point toward a genuine redesign, it's worth knowing roughly what that process looks like before starting, so expectations are set properly. A proper redesign typically moves through defining goals and target audience, planning the site's structure and content before any visual design begins, designing and reviewing the new look, building the site on the chosen platform, migrating and updating content, testing across devices and browsers, and then launching with attention to preserving existing SEO rankings during the transition (redirects from old URLs to new ones being a critical, easily overlooked step here).

It's worth being direct about one thing: a redesign doesn't automatically increase conversions or traffic on its own. The result depends heavily on the quality of the new design, the clarity of the messaging, the offer itself, the traffic actually arriving at the site, and how well the user experience and conversion flow are genuinely thought through — not simply on the site looking newer. A beautifully redesigned site with a confusing structure or an unclear offer can convert just as poorly as the old one did, just with better visuals.

Accessibility is worth folding into this process too, even though it doesn't always make it onto a business owner's initial checklist. A site with poor colour contrast, images missing descriptive alt text, or a layout that's difficult to navigate using a keyboard alone isn't just excluding some visitors unnecessarily — in many markets it's also increasingly a factor search engines and some regulations take into account. A redesign is a natural, cost-effective point to build these considerations in from the start, rather than retrofitting them later.

Security and long-term maintainability are two more factors that quietly justify a redesign even when none of the five visible signs feel urgent yet. An old platform that's stopped receiving security updates, or a site running on outdated software versions, carries growing risk the longer it's left as-is — and a redesign on a modern, actively maintained platform resolves that risk while also making future content updates and small feature additions considerably easier to handle without specialist help every time.

How Long Should a Redesign Take, and What Should You Expect to Provide?

Timelines vary widely depending on the size of the site and how much content needs to be created or migrated, but it's useful to know roughly what phases are involved so a redesign doesn't feel like an opaque black box once it starts.

Early on, expect a discovery phase where goals, target audience, and the specific problems from your self-audit get discussed and prioritised — this is the point to be candid about which of the five signs matter most to you and why, so the new site is actually built to solve those, not just to look different. A planning phase follows, mapping out the site's structure and content before any visual design work begins, since starting with visuals before structure tends to produce a site that looks good but doesn't actually organise information the way visitors need.

Design and build phases come next, typically with review checkpoints along the way rather than a single reveal at the end — this is where your input matters most, since catching a structural or messaging issue during design review is far cheaper than catching it after the site is built. Content migration and updates happen in parallel or shortly after, followed by testing across devices, browsers, and — critically — actual mobile phones, not just browser resizing.

One step that's easy to underestimate in importance: preserving SEO value during launch. If your current site ranks for any meaningful search terms, the redesign needs a plan for redirecting old URLs to their new equivalents, or that ranking history can be lost in the transition — sometimes significantly, and sometimes not until weeks after launch when the effect becomes visible in traffic. This is worth asking about directly before a redesign begins, not after.

On your side, expect to need to provide or confirm current business information (services, pricing, team details), any brand assets you have (logo files, existing photography), and — most importantly — time for review at each checkpoint, since redesigns tend to stall when review and feedback get delayed on the client's side rather than the development side.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Redesigning purely for a fresher look without addressing the underlying speed or mobile issues that were actually driving visitors away — the site ends up looking nicer but performing just as poorly.
  • Leaving outdated content in place through a redesign, effectively carrying old, misleading information into a shiny new wrapper.
  • Assuming a full redesign is needed when a handful of targeted fixes would have solved the actual problem for a fraction of the cost and time.
  • Skipping the audit step entirely and jumping straight to "let's redesign the whole thing" based on a vague feeling rather than specific, testable signs.
  • Not planning for SEO continuity during a redesign, leading to a real (and often significant) drop in search visibility right after launch due to broken URLs or lost content.
