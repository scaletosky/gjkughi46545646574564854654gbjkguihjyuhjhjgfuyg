How to Do Keyword Research Without a Paid Tool

A practical free-tool keyword research process good enough to inform real content decisions.

By Scale To Sky Team·Published 9 September 2026·22 min read

Quick Answer

Before going through the process, it's worth being upfront about what free tools can and can't do, because a lot of "free keyword research" content online oversells the results. What free tools are genuinely good at: surfacing real phrases people actually search, showing you related questions and topics you might not have thought of, revealing roughly how competitive a search term looks by showing you who currently ranks for it, and helping you understand search intent — whether someone typing a phrase wants information, wants to compare options, or is ready to buy or book. What free tools are weak at: giving you precise monthly search volume numbers.

Key Takeaways
  • Pick a seed keyword relevant to your business
  • Mine autocomplete and the alphabet trick for real long-tail variations
  • Pull question ideas from People Also Ask and related searches
  • Check Google Keyword Planner (free, no active campaign required) for directional volume
  • Use Google Trends to compare similar keyword phrasings and check seasonality

Yes, you can do genuinely useful keyword research without paying for Ahrefs, SEMrush, or any other subscription tool — with one honest caveat: free methods won't give you the precise search-volume numbers those paid tools provide. What they will give you is something arguably more important when you're starting out: a clear picture of what your actual customers are typing into Google, how competitive those searches currently look, and which ones are realistic to target first. This article walks through exactly how to do that, step by step, using tools that are already free and built into Google.

This is written for small business owners, freelancers, and anyone managing their own website or blog who knows keyword research matters but can't yet justify ₹8,000–₹15,000+ a month for a premium SEO tool subscription. You don't need one to get started, and in many cases you don't need one at all until your content operation is large enough that saving research time becomes worth more than the subscription cost.

Can Free Keyword Research Actually Work? Short Answer: Yes, With Limits

Before going through the process, it's worth being upfront about what free tools can and can't do, because a lot of "free keyword research" content online oversells the results.

What free tools are genuinely good at: surfacing real phrases people actually search, showing you related questions and topics you might not have thought of, revealing roughly how competitive a search term looks by showing you who currently ranks for it, and helping you understand search intent — whether someone typing a phrase wants information, wants to compare options, or is ready to buy or book.

What free tools are weak at: giving you precise monthly search volume numbers. Google's own free-tier tools show volume as a range (for example, "1K–10K" rather than an exact figure), and there's no reliable free way to get the kind of precise, month-by-month volume trends that a paid tool provides. This matters less than it sounds like it should, though — for most small businesses picking their first handful of target keywords, knowing that a term gets "meaningful search volume" and is realistically competitive is often enough to make a good decision, even without knowing whether the true number is 800 or 1,200 searches a month.

What Keyword Research Actually Means (Beyond Just "Finding Words")

Before getting into specific tools, it helps to be clear about what you're actually trying to figure out, because keyword research isn't really about collecting a list of words — it's about understanding your potential customers' actual language and intent well enough to create content that matches what they're looking for.

Search Intent Comes Before the Keyword Itself

Every search falls roughly into one of a few intent categories, and understanding which one you're dealing with changes what kind of page or content should target it. Informational searches ("what is local SEO," "how does Instagram algorithm work") are from people looking to learn something, not necessarily buy yet. Commercial or comparison searches ("best web development company in Lucknow," "Wordpress vs custom website") are from people comparing options before deciding. Transactional searches ("hire digital marketing agency Lucknow," "book photographer near me") are from people close to taking action. Navigational searches (someone typing a specific business or brand name) are from people who already know exactly where they want to go.

Matching content type to intent matters more than most beginners realize. Targeting a transactional, ready-to-buy keyword with a long, general blog post about the topic tends to underperform a focused service page, while targeting an informational keyword with a hard-sell landing page tends to frustrate visitors who wanted to learn something first, not be pitched to immediately.

Seed Keywords Are Your Starting Point, Not Your Final List

A seed keyword is simply a broad, obvious term related to what you do — "digital marketing agency," "web development," "cake delivery." On their own, seed keywords are usually too competitive and too broad to target directly, especially for a newer or smaller website. Their real value is as a starting point that you expand outward from using the free methods below, gradually narrowing toward the more specific phrases your actual customers use.

Think in Terms of Your Customer's Actual Language, Not Industry Language

Businesses often default to the terminology they use internally, which doesn't always match how customers actually search. A business that calls itself a "full-service digital marketing agency" might find that potential customers are searching "who can manage my Instagram" or "someone to run my Facebook ads" — much more specific, much more human phrasing. Every method below is partly about surfacing this real customer language rather than assuming your internal vocabulary matches the market's.

Tool 1: Google Autocomplete — The Fastest Free Method

Autocomplete is the dropdown of suggestions that appears as you type into Google's search bar, and it's genuinely one of the most useful free keyword research tools available, precisely because every suggestion reflects something real people have actually searched.

How to Use It Properly

Start by typing a seed keyword relevant to your business — for example, "digital marketing agency in" — and note every suggestion that appears. Then try variations: adding "for" ("social media management for"), adding "near" ("website development company near"), or adding a question word before the seed ("how to choose a digital marketing agency"). Each variation surfaces a different set of suggestions, because Google's autocomplete responds differently depending on the exact phrasing and word order.

The Alphabet Trick for Deeper Coverage

A useful technique for surfacing more suggestions than the default handful is what's often called the "alphabet trick" — after typing your seed phrase, add a space and then each letter of the alphabet one at a time (your seed phrase + "a", then + "b", then + "c", and so on), noting what autocomplete suggests for each. This surfaces long-tail variations you'd likely never think of on your own, because it's showing you actual patterns in what real people search, not just the most common single suggestion.

For example, a bakery researching "cake delivery in Lucknow" might discover through this method that people also commonly search "eggless cake delivery in Lucknow," a specific, high-intent variation that's both more targetable (less competitive than the generic term) and directly relevant if the bakery actually offers eggless options — a much better opportunity than trying to compete head-on for the broader, more generic "cake shop" search.

Tool 2: "People Also Ask" and Related Searches

Once you search any keyword on Google, two features on the results page are genuinely rich, free sources of real keyword and content ideas: the "People Also Ask" box (a set of expandable questions, usually appearing partway down the results) and the "Related searches" section (a list of related queries, usually near the bottom of the page).

Mining the People Also Ask Box

Each question in the People Also Ask box represents a real, common question people search around your topic. Clicking one question typically reveals more related questions beneath it, letting you go several layers deep into related queries you might not have considered. For a business writing content around, say, "social media management," the PAA box might surface questions like "is social media management worth it for small business" or "how much does social media management cost" — both genuinely useful signals for what content to create, since they represent real hesitations or questions potential customers already have.

Related Searches for Broader Coverage

The related searches section, typically found near the bottom of the results page, tends to show a wider spread of related topics rather than specifically question-based phrasing. This is useful for spotting adjacent topics and subtopics you might not have considered targeting at all, expanding your understanding of the full topic area rather than just one specific keyword.

Tool 3: Google Keyword Planner (Free Tier)

Google Keyword Planner is a legitimately free tool, though it requires a Google Ads account to access — you do not need to be running active paid campaigns or spending any money to use it, despite this being a common point of confusion.

Setting It Up

Creating a Google Ads account takes a few minutes and requires basic business information, but you can skip past the "create your first campaign" step (usually by looking for an option like "switch to expert mode" during setup) without ever launching a paid campaign. Once inside the Google Ads dashboard, Keyword Planner is typically found under the Tools and Settings menu, in the Planning section.

What You Get and What You Don't

Inside Keyword Planner, you can enter seed keywords or a URL and get a list of related keyword ideas, along with a volume figure for each. The important caveat: without an active, spending campaign history, that volume figure is typically shown as a broad range (for example, "1K–10K" per month) rather than a precise number. This is intentional on Google's part and applies to all accounts without meaningful ad spend, not a bug or a limitation specific to your account.

Despite the range-based data, Keyword Planner is still genuinely useful at the free tier for two reasons: it reveals related keyword ideas you might not have found through autocomplete or PAA alone, and the ranges themselves are still directionally useful — a keyword showing "10K–100K" clearly gets meaningfully more search interest than one showing "10–100," even without knowing the exact number in between.

Tool 4: Google Trends — Comparing Relative Interest

Google Trends is a free, public tool that shows relative search interest for a term over time, and its main value for keyword research is comparison rather than absolute numbers.

Comparing Keyword Variations

If you're unsure which of two similar phrases to prioritize — say, "app development company" versus "mobile app developer" — Google Trends lets you enter both and see their relative popularity over time on the same chart. This won't tell you the exact search volume of either, but it will tell you which one is more commonly searched relative to the other, which is often exactly the decision you're trying to make.

Spotting Seasonal Patterns

Trends is also useful for spotting seasonal fluctuation that a snapshot volume number wouldn't reveal. A search like "wedding photographer" or "banquet hall booking" likely shows a clear seasonal pattern tied to wedding season in a given region, which is useful to know when planning content or ad timing — publishing or promoting content right before a seasonal peak tends to perform better than publishing reactively once the peak has already started.

Filtering by Region

Trends allows filtering by country and, in many cases, by state or region, which is particularly useful for local businesses trying to understand demand specifically in their market rather than nationally — search interest in "digital marketing agency" nationally might look very different from interest specifically within Uttar Pradesh or a specific city.

Tool 5: Your Own Google Search Console (If You Already Have Content)

If your website has been live for a while and has any existing content, Google Search Console — a free tool directly from Google that shows how your site actually performs in search — is one of the most underused free keyword research sources available, precisely because it shows real data about your own site rather than general estimates.

Finding "Almost Ranking" Keywords

Inside Search Console's Performance report, you can see the actual queries your existing pages are getting impressions for, along with their average position. This often reveals keywords you're already ranking for on page two (positions 11–20) that are close enough to page one that a targeted content update — adding a missing subtopic, improving the page's depth, or building a few relevant internal links — could plausibly push them onto page one, where the majority of clicks happen.

Discovering Keywords You Didn't Intentionally Target

Search Console frequently surfaces queries a page is getting impressions for that you never deliberately optimized around, revealing genuine gaps or opportunities in your existing content — sometimes an entire subtopic worth its own dedicated page, rather than being covered only briefly within a broader article.

How to Judge Competition Without a Paid Difficulty Score

Paid tools typically provide a "keyword difficulty" score, a single number estimating how hard a keyword is to rank for. Without that score, you can still get a reasonably reliable read on competition by manually checking who currently occupies the search results.

Manually Reviewing the Top 10

Search the keyword yourself and look honestly at what's currently ranking. If the top results are dominated by large, well-established, high-authority sites — major publications, national brands, long-running category leaders — that's a signal the keyword is genuinely difficult to compete for as a newer or smaller site, at least in the near term. If the results include smaller local businesses, less polished content, or pages that clearly aren't comprehensively answering the query, that's a signal there may be a realistic opening.

Favoring Long-Tail, More Specific Phrases as a Beginner

Long-tail keywords — longer, more specific phrases like "affordable website development company in Lucknow for small business" rather than just "website development" — are consistently easier to compete for than short, broad, generic terms, simply because fewer sites are specifically optimized around the exact, longer phrase. They also tend to convert better, since a searcher using a more specific phrase usually knows more precisely what they want, which typically also means they're closer to making a decision.

Checking Search Result Features, Not Just Organic Listings

It's also worth noting what else appears on the results page beyond the ten blue links — a prominent local map pack (for location-based searches), a shopping carousel, or a heavily featured "People Also Ask" box all change how much organic click-through you can realistically expect even if you do rank well, which is useful context when deciding where to invest your effort.

Local Keyword Research: Service + Location Patterns

For most small businesses — especially service businesses serving a specific city or region — local keyword research deserves its own specific attention, because the patterns and priorities differ somewhat from general keyword research.

The Core Pattern: Service + Location

The large majority of useful local search phrases follow a simple service-plus-location pattern: "restaurant in Lucknow," "best banquet hall in Lucknow," "digital marketing agency in Lucknow," "website development company in Lucknow," "social media management for restaurants." Running each of your core services through this pattern, combined with your city and any relevant neighborhoods, is often the fastest way to build an initial local keyword list.

Don't Over-Optimize the Exact Phrase

While the service-plus-location pattern is a useful starting point for understanding what people search, it's a mistake to then cram that exact phrase repeatedly throughout a page's content in an attempt to "match" it precisely. Google's understanding of relevance has moved well beyond exact phrase matching — a page that naturally, clearly, and thoroughly covers the service and clearly indicates the relevant location (in the title, headings, and naturally within the content) performs better than one that repeats the identical phrase unnaturally throughout.

Location Modifiers Beyond Just City Name

Depending on the business, useful location modifiers can go beyond just the city — specific neighborhoods, "near me" style searches (which Google increasingly matches based on the searcher's actual location rather than the literal words), or nearby landmark references for very local businesses. Checking autocomplete with your service plus a few different neighborhood names can reveal whether there's meaningful search volume at that more granular level.

Informational Keywords

These reflect a desire to learn or understand something — "what is local SEO," "how does retargeting work," "why is my Instagram not growing." These are typically best served by blog-style content that genuinely answers the question in depth, rather than a service page, since someone at this stage usually isn't ready to buy yet.

Commercial Investigation Keywords

These reflect someone comparing options before deciding — "best digital marketing agency in Lucknow," "Wordpress vs custom website," "SEO vs paid ads for small business." Comparison-style content, honest pros-and-cons breakdowns, and content that helps someone make a confident decision tend to perform well here, and this stage is often where trust gets built before a purchase decision.

Transactional Keywords

These reflect someone ready or close to ready to act — "hire digital marketing agency Lucknow," "book photographer Lucknow," "website development company near me." These are usually best served by a focused service or landing page with a clear call-to-action, rather than a long educational blog post, since the visitor has largely already made their decision and mainly needs a fast, easy path to take action.

Keyword Clustering: Grouping Related Terms Instead of Treating Each One Separately

Once you've gathered a reasonably long list of keyword ideas, a common beginner mistake is trying to create a separate page for every single variation. In reality, many keyword variations are close enough in meaning that Google will treat them as essentially the same search intent, and a single well-built page can realistically rank for dozens of closely related phrases simultaneously.

Keyword clustering means grouping semantically similar keywords together — "digital marketing agency Lucknow," "best digital marketing company Lucknow," and "digital marketing services Lucknow" likely belong on the same page rather than three separate pages, since they reflect essentially the same search intent with minor phrasing differences. Trying to create separate pages for each close variation typically leads to keyword cannibalization, covered next, rather than better rankings.

Avoiding Keyword Cannibalization

Keyword cannibalization happens when two or more pages on the same website are both trying to rank for the same, or very similar, keyword — which typically doesn't help your chances of ranking for either page and can actually hurt both, since it splits relevance signals and sometimes confuses Google about which page to actually rank for the term.

Before creating a new page around a keyword, it's worth checking whether an existing page on your site already covers similar ground. If it does, the better move is usually strengthening that existing page (making it more comprehensive, more current, better structured) rather than creating a competing new page — and if you do have two overlapping pages already, consolidating them into one stronger page, or clearly differentiating their angles and target keywords, tends to work better than leaving them to compete against each other indefinitely.

Prioritizing Keywords: What to Target First

With a list of keyword ideas gathered and roughly categorized, the next step is deciding what to actually target first, since most small businesses can't realistically create content around every keyword idea at once.

A Practical Prioritization Framework

A useful, simple way to prioritize without paid difficulty scores is weighing three factors together: relevance (how directly does this keyword connect to something you actually offer), realistic competition (based on your manual top-10 review, does this look achievable for a site at your current stage), and business value (if you did rank and someone found this page, how likely are they to actually become a customer, versus just a casual visitor).

A keyword can score well on relevance and business value but poorly on realistic competition, in which case it's often worth targeting a more specific, longer-tail version of the same idea first, and revisiting the broader term once your site has built more authority. A keyword with low competition but weak relevance to your actual business, on the other hand, usually isn't worth the effort even if it looks easy to rank for, since ranking for it wouldn't meaningfully help the business anyway.

Choosing Keywords for Individual Pages

Each important page on your site — service pages especially — should generally have one clear primary keyword it's built around, with a small number of closely related secondary keywords woven in naturally. Trying to make a single page target too many unrelated keywords at once usually produces vague, unfocused content that doesn't fully satisfy any single search intent particularly well.

Putting It Together: A Simple Free Keyword Research Workflow

Rather than treating each tool above as a separate exercise, they work best combined into a single, repeatable process:

  1. Start with a seed keyword directly relevant to the service or topic you want to target.
  2. Expand it using autocomplete and the alphabet trick to surface real long-tail variations and customer language.
  3. Mine People Also Ask and related searches for question-based ideas and adjacent subtopics.
  4. Check Google Keyword Planner for additional keyword ideas and a directional sense of volume, even if only shown as a range.
  5. Use Google Trends as a sanity check when deciding between two or more similar keyword phrasings.
  6. Manually review the top 10 results for your shortlisted keywords to gauge realistic competition.
  7. Cluster related keywords together and assign each cluster to one page, avoiding overlap with existing content.
  8. Pick three to five realistic target keywords to start with, weighing relevance, competition, and business value together.

Keeping this list in a simple spreadsheet — even just columns for keyword, intent type, rough competition notes, and which page it's assigned to — is usually all the "tooling" a small business genuinely needs to run this process consistently, without any paid software involved.

Common Mistakes Beginners Make With Free Keyword Research

  • Only targeting broad, one-word or two-word keywords with intense competition. Generic terms like "digital marketing" or "web design" are dominated by large, established sites, making them a poor first target for a smaller or newer website.
  • Ignoring long-tail, question-based phrases. These are usually easier to rank for and often convert better, since they reflect more specific, closer-to-decision intent.
  • Never checking who currently ranks before writing. Skipping the manual top-10 review means investing time in content for a keyword that may be unrealistic to compete for, at least in the near term.
  • Treating every keyword idea as worth its own page. This leads to thin, overlapping content and keyword cannibalization rather than a smaller number of genuinely strong, comprehensive pages.
  • Chasing volume alone, ignoring relevance. A high-volume keyword that only loosely relates to what you actually offer rarely converts well, even if it does drive traffic.
  • Stuffing the exact keyword phrase unnaturally throughout the content. This reads poorly to actual readers and doesn't align with how modern search engines evaluate relevance.

Turning Research Into Actual Content

Keyword research on its own doesn't produce results — it only becomes useful once it shapes actual pages and content. A practical way to bridge the two: for each prioritized keyword cluster, note the specific intent (informational, commercial, or transactional), then sketch out what a genuinely thorough answer to that intent would include — the questions a real reader would have, in the order they'd naturally think of them, based partly on the People Also Ask and related searches data gathered earlier.

This tends to produce far more useful content than starting from a blank page and guessing what to write, because the structure is grounded in questions real people are actually asking, rather than what you assume they might want to know.

When It's Worth Upgrading to a Paid Tool

Free methods are genuinely sufficient for most small businesses just getting started, but there's a reasonably clear point where a paid tool starts saving more time and money than it costs. If you're publishing content regularly (multiple pieces a month) and spending significant hours manually cross-referencing autocomplete, PAA, and Keyword Planner ranges for each one, a paid tool's precise volume data, difficulty scoring, and bulk research features can meaningfully speed up a process you're already doing manually. Similarly, if you're managing keyword strategy across many pages or multiple client websites at once, the time saved by paid tools' more efficient interfaces and bulk features often justifies the subscription cost in a way it wouldn't for someone publishing occasionally.

The honest takeaway: paid tools don't unlock a fundamentally different or better research process — they largely speed up and add precision to the same underlying process covered in this article. Starting with free tools isn't a lesser approach; it's often the right approach until your volume of work genuinely justifies the time savings a subscription provides.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Google Keyword Planner really free to use?

Yes, it's accessible with a free Google Ads account even without running paid campaigns, though volume data is shown in ranges rather than exact numbers on the free tier without meaningful ad spend history.

Can free keyword research really replace paid tools like Ahrefs or SEMrush?

For small businesses just starting out, free methods can identify solid, relevant keyword opportunities without any subscription cost. Paid tools become more valuable at scale, when precise volume and competition data start saving significant time across a larger volume of content or client work.

How do I know if a keyword is too competitive for a new website?

Manually checking who currently ranks in the top 10 gives a good signal — if it's dominated by large, established sites, a more specific, longer-tail variation is usually a better starting target than trying to compete head-on for the broader term.

What's the easiest free method to start with?

Google autocomplete and the People Also Ask box are the simplest starting points, since they directly reflect real searches and real questions people are actually making, with no setup required beyond a normal Google search.

Do I need to know coding or technical SEO to do this?

No — everything covered in this article is done directly inside a normal browser, using Google's own search results and free tools. Technical SEO (site speed, structured data, and similar factors) matters separately, but keyword research itself doesn't require any technical skill.

How many keywords should I target per page?

Generally one clear primary keyword per page, supported by a handful of closely related secondary keywords and natural variations woven in where they genuinely fit. Trying to target many unrelated keywords on a single page tends to produce vague content that doesn't fully satisfy any one search intent.
