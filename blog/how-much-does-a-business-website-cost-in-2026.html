How Much Does a Business Website Cost in 2026

A clear understanding of cost drivers and realistic tiers so they can budget and evaluate quotes sensibly.

"How much does a website cost?" is one of the most searched, and most frustrating, questions a business owner can ask, because the honest answer is always some version of "it depends." That's not a dodge — it's genuinely true, and the range in 2026 is wide enough that a simple landing page and a full custom e-commerce platform can differ in price by a factor of fifty or more.

This article won't hand you one number and call it a day, because any article that does is either guessing or trying to sell you something without knowing what you actually need. Instead, it walks through exactly what drives website cost, what realistic tiers look like, and how to get an accurate quote for your specific situation — so that when quotes start coming in, you understand what you're actually comparing.

Why Website Costs Vary So Widely

Before looking at any numbers, it helps to understand why the range is so large in the first place. A website isn't a single, standardized product the way a laptop or a car is — it's closer to construction. "Build me a house" produces wildly different quotes depending on square footage, materials, custom features, and finish quality, and a website works the same way.

The biggest swing factors are: whether the site is built on a template/CMS platform or coded custom from scratch, how many pages and how much custom functionality it needs, whether it's a simple informational site or a full e-commerce platform with inventory and payments, and how much original content — copywriting, photography, video — needs to be created as part of the project versus already existing. Two businesses can both ask for "a website" and end up needing genuinely different amounts of work once these factors are accounted for.

It's worth being upfront that this article was researched with current market data, but website pricing shifts with the market, with agency rates, and with the specific vendor you talk to — so treat every figure here as a general orientation for budgeting purposes, not a quote, and always confirm actual pricing with vendors directly for your specific project.

The Main Cost Drivers

Number of pages and custom functionality. A five-page brochure site (home, about, services, contact, and one more) is a fundamentally smaller project than a thirty-page site with a blog, multiple service pages, a resource library, and custom forms for different inquiry types. Beyond simple pages, functionality like booking systems, membership logins, multi-step forms, or custom calculators all add development time on top of the base page count.

 

Custom design vs template-based build. Starting from a professional, pre-built template and customizing colors, fonts, and layout to match your brand is significantly faster and cheaper than a fully custom design built from a blank canvas around your specific brand and user experience goals. Templates aren't inherently bad — many professional, fast, good-looking small business sites are built on well-chosen templates — but a fully bespoke design, with original layouts and interactions designed specifically for your business, costs more because it requires more design hours before any development even starts.

 

E-commerce vs informational site. A site that simply presents information about your business is a fundamentally different (and usually cheaper) build than one that needs to handle a product catalog, shopping cart, payment gateway integration, inventory management, order tracking, and often shipping calculations. E-commerce functionality adds real complexity regardless of platform, because payments and inventory both need to work correctly and securely, with no room for the kind of small bugs that might be tolerable on a purely informational page.

 

Content creation — copywriting, photography, video. A quote for "building" a website often assumes you're providing the actual words and images, and a lot of confusion and scope creep happens when that assumption turns out to be wrong partway through a project. If you need professional copywriting for every page, original photography of your business, products, or team, or video content, that's meaningful additional work (and often a different skill set from the development itself) that should be budgeted and scoped separately from the build.

With those cost drivers in mind, here's a general sense of how projects tend to cluster by complexity. These are illustrative tiers meant to help you recognize roughly where your own project sits — not a menu with fixed prices, since actual quotes always depend on your specific scope, chosen vendor, and location.

Mid-complexity site10-25 pages, booking or inquiry forms, blog, some custom design elements, integrations like WhatsApp or calendarModerate

Current market research on small business website pricing (both in India and internationally) consistently shows the same pattern: a straightforward brochure-style site for a local service business tends to sit at the lower end of a market's typical range, while sites needing custom functionality, e-commerce, or a fully bespoke design climb from there, sometimes substantially. For an Indian small business, a straightforward, professionally built 5-15 page CMS-based site with mobile-responsive design and basic SEO commonly falls in the tens of thousands of rupees range from a reliable agency or experienced freelancer, while a fully custom-coded site or a full e-commerce platform with inventory and payment integration typically runs well into six figures (in rupees) or beyond, depending on scope. These are directional ranges based on current market patterns, not guaranteed prices — always get a scope-specific quote for your actual project rather than budgeting purely off a range you read online.

Design Quality and Why It Affects Price So Much

Design is one of the least understood cost drivers, partly because it's genuinely hard to compare two design quotes without seeing the actual work. Two vendors can both say "custom design included" and mean very different amounts of actual design effort behind that phrase.

At a basic level, a template-adaptation approach means a designer takes an existing, professionally built layout and adjusts colors, fonts, spacing, and a handful of elements to roughly match your brand. This is genuinely fast, and for many small businesses, entirely sufficient — a well-chosen template, adapted thoughtfully, can look completely professional and load quickly.

A fully custom design process, by contrast, typically starts from your brand identity and actual user goals, and builds a layout, visual hierarchy, and set of interactions specifically for your business — often including wireframing (rough structural sketches before visual design begins), one or more rounds of visual design concepts, and revision cycles based on your feedback. This process takes meaningfully more design hours, which is reflected directly in cost. It's usually the right investment when your brand identity is a genuine differentiator in a competitive market, or when a generic look would actively undersell a premium product or service. It's often not necessary for a straightforward local service business where customers primarily care about clear information, credibility, and ease of contacting you.

Responsive design — the site adapting cleanly to phones, tablets, and desktops — is worth calling out separately because in 2026 it's not an optional add-on; the overwhelming majority of website visits for most small businesses come from mobile devices, and a site that isn't genuinely well-optimized for mobile (not just "technically viewable," but easy to navigate, read, and act on with a thumb) is actively costing you visitors and customers regardless of how good it looks on a desktop monitor. Any quote you receive should include real mobile responsiveness as a baseline expectation, not a premium feature.

Payment Systems, Forms, and Admin Requirements

Beyond the core pages, a few functional pieces are worth budgeting for explicitly, since they're easy to underestimate when scoping a project.

Payment gateway integration. If your site needs to accept payments — for products, bookings, or deposits — integrating a payment gateway (Razorpay, PayU, Stripe, and similar providers are common choices in the Indian market) adds development work beyond a purely informational site, along with transaction fees charged by the payment provider itself, separate from your website development cost. Getting this right also matters for security and compliance, which is part of why e-commerce sites generally cost more than informational ones.

 

Forms and inquiry systems. A basic "name, email, message" contact form is inexpensive to build. Multi-step forms, forms that route different inquiry types to different people, forms with file uploads, or forms integrated with a CRM or email marketing tool all add complexity proportional to how much logic sits behind them.

 

Admin/content management needs. If you'll need to update pricing, add blog posts, upload new products, or change photos regularly after launch, it's worth explicitly discussing how easy that will be for a non-technical person on your team to do without calling the developer every time. A well-built CMS setup should let you make routine updates yourself; a poorly scoped custom build sometimes locks you into needing developer help for even small changes, which becomes a hidden ongoing cost of its own.

WordPress/Template vs Custom-Coded: How This Affects Cost

One of the more consequential early decisions is whether your site runs on a content management system like WordPress (or a similar platform) versus being coded entirely custom. This choice affects both upfront cost and how the site behaves for years afterward.

WordPress and comparable CMS platforms remain a genuinely sensible default for most small and medium business websites. They're faster to build (since a huge amount of underlying functionality — page structure, media handling, plugin ecosystems for forms, SEO, and more — already exists rather than needing to be built from scratch), generally more affordable, and let a non-technical business owner make basic content updates themselves afterward without needing a developer for every small text or image change.

Custom-coded websites, built without a general-purpose CMS underneath, offer more control over exactly how the site behaves and performs, and can be the right call when a business has genuinely unusual requirements a CMS and its plugin ecosystem can't handle cleanly, or when very specific performance or security requirements matter enough to justify the additional cost. For most brochure-style or moderately complex business sites, though, a well-built CMS site delivers the vast majority of what a business actually needs, at meaningfully lower cost. There's a full breakdown of this decision, including when custom coding genuinely earns its higher price, in WordPress vs Custom-Coded Website — Which Is Better for You.

Hidden and Ongoing Costs Beyond the Build

A number that only covers the initial build isn't the full picture of what a website costs, and this is one of the most common sources of budget surprise for first-time website buyers. A few costs that continue after launch, and are easy to overlook when comparing initial quotes:

  • Domain registration. A relatively small annual cost, but a recurring one that needs to be renewed to keep your website address active.
  • Hosting. The server space that actually runs your website, billed monthly or annually, with cost scaling based on the traffic and resources your site needs.
  • SSL certificate. Needed for the secure "https" padlock in browsers; often bundled with hosting but occasionally a separate line item.
  • Maintenance and updates. CMS platforms, plugins, and themes need periodic updates for security and compatibility; some agencies include a maintenance period in the initial quote, others price it separately as an ongoing plan.
  • Future content changes. Adding new pages, updating copy, or refreshing photography after launch is usually billed separately from the original build unless specifically agreed upon in advance.

None of these individually tends to be large, but added together over a year, they represent a real recurring cost on top of the initial build — and it's worth asking any vendor directly what happens after handover: who owns the hosting account, who's responsible for updates, and what a basic content change costs if you need one six months after launch.

SEO Foundations: What Should Be Included From the Start

Search engine optimization is sometimes treated as a separate, later purchase from the website build itself — something you do "after" the site is live, as its own project. Some level of ongoing SEO work (content creation, link building, keyword tracking) genuinely is an ongoing activity beyond the initial build. But a meaningful set of SEO foundations should be part of the build itself, not bolted on afterward, because retrofitting them later is more work than including them from the start.

These foundations include: clean, sensible page titles and meta descriptions for every page rather than generic defaults, a logical site structure and internal linking that helps both visitors and search engines understand how your pages relate to each other, reasonably fast page load times (which depend partly on how the site is built and partly on image optimization), proper heading structure (a single clear H1 per page, sensibly nested H2s and H3s below it), and basic technical setup like an XML sitemap and correct mobile rendering. None of this guarantees ranking well — genuine SEO success also depends on content quality, competition, and consistent effort over time — but a site built without these foundations starts from a real handicap that has to be fixed later, usually at additional cost, before any further SEO work can be effective. If ranking on Google is a genuine priority for your business, it's worth asking any web development vendor directly whether these foundations are included in their standard build or treated as a separate add-on.

Content and Photography: Often the Most Underestimated Line Item

It's easy to focus entirely on design and development when budgeting for a website and forget that someone still has to write every page's copy and supply every image. This is one of the most commonly underestimated parts of a website project, for two reasons: it's not always clearly itemized in an initial quote, and business owners often assume they'll "just write it themselves" without realizing how much time that actually takes once the project is underway.

If you're providing your own copy, it's worth being realistic about the time commitment — writing clear, effective copy for even a modest ten-page site is genuinely a few days of focused work for someone comfortable writing, and considerably longer for someone who isn't. Delays in delivering content are one of the most common reasons website projects run past their planned timeline, since a developer generally can't finalize page layouts without knowing roughly how much text needs to fit.

Photography carries a similar consideration. Stock photography is a low-cost, fast option that works reasonably well for many small business sites, but it can also make a site feel generic if the images don't actually reflect your specific business, team, or physical space. Original photography — of your storefront, products, team, or work in progress — tends to build more trust and feels more authentic, but requires either budgeting for a professional photographer or being comfortable with reasonably good phone photography, and either way, it needs to be planned for as its own task with its own timeline, not assumed to simply appear when needed.

Cheap Website vs Expensive Website: What's the Real Difference

It's a fair question: if two developers both promise to build "a website for my business," why is one quote three times another? The honest answer is that they're often not actually promising the same thing, even though it sounds like it on the surface.

A lower-priced build often means: a heavily templated design with minimal customization, limited or no dedicated attention to on-page SEO fundamentals (page titles, meta descriptions, image optimization, site structure), less rigorous testing across devices and browsers, and less ongoing support built into the price. None of that automatically makes a cheaper option a bad choice — for a genuinely simple, low-stakes site, it might be exactly the right amount of investment. But it's worth going in with eyes open about what's typically excluded at a lower price point, rather than assuming every quote covers the same scope of work.

A higher-priced build, when the additional cost is genuinely justified rather than just marked up, usually reflects: more design hours spent on a layout and visual identity specific to your brand rather than a generic template, more attention to search engine fundamentals baked in from the start rather than bolted on later, more thorough testing, better ongoing support, and often faster loading performance from more careful technical work. The honest test for whether a higher price is worth it isn't the number itself — it's whether you can get a clear answer about what specifically that additional cost buys you, and whether those things actually matter for your business.

How to Get an Accurate Quote

The single most effective thing you can do to get comparable, accurate quotes from multiple vendors is to prepare a clear, written scope before you start asking for prices. This doesn't need to be a formal document — a simple list is enough — but it should cover:

  • An approximate list of pages you need (home, about, services, contact, blog, etc.)
  • Any specific functionality required (booking forms, product catalog, payment processing, member login, multilingual support)
  • Whether you're providing content (copy, photos) or need it created as part of the project
  • Examples of two or three existing websites whose look, feel, or functionality you like, and briefly why
  • A rough budget range, even a wide one, so vendors can tell you early whether your expectations and their pricing are roughly compatible
  • Any hard deadline, if one genuinely exists

Bringing this to three different vendors — a mix of freelancers and agencies, if you're weighing that decision too — makes it far easier to compare quotes on an apples-to-apples basis, since you're now asking everyone to price the same scope rather than letting each vendor guess at what you need and price their own assumption of it.

Agency vs Freelancer vs DIY: A Quick Comparison

RouteBest FitTrade-off to Know
DIY builder (Wix, Squarespace, etc.)Very simple sites, extremely tight budgets, comfort doing it yourselfLowest cost, but limited customization and you do all the work yourself
FreelancerClearly scoped, bounded projects with a defined finish lineOften more affordable than an agency, but less structure and backup if the freelancer is unavailable
AgencyProjects needing strategy, design, development, and ongoing support togetherHigher cost, generally more accountability, process, and continuity

None of these three is universally "correct" — a very simple project with a tight budget and a comfortable, hands-on owner might be perfectly well served by a DIY builder, while a business planning meaningful growth and needing a dependable long-term partner for a more complex site is often better served by an agency relationship, even at a higher price, because of the accountability and continuity that comes with it. It's also worth being realistic about your own available time: a DIY builder shifts a real amount of work — design decisions, troubleshooting, ongoing updates — onto you personally, which is a genuine cost even though it doesn't appear as a rupee figure anywhere.

Freelancers are worth a specific word of caution, not because they're a worse choice, but because outcomes vary more widely than with an established agency. A skilled, experienced freelancer with a solid portfolio and clear communication can deliver excellent work at a lower price than an agency, precisely because there's less overhead. But a freelancer relationship also concentrates all the risk in one person — if they become unavailable partway through the project, or after launch when you need a fix, there's no larger team to fall back on the way there often is with an agency. Checking references, past client feedback, and asking directly how they handle unexpected unavailability are reasonable questions to ask before committing.

Common Cost Mistakes

Choosing the cheapest option without checking what's excluded. The lowest quote sometimes turns out to exclude things you assumed were included — SEO setup, mobile optimization, revisions, hosting setup — leading to unexpected additional costs once the project is underway. Always ask directly what's included and what would cost extra.

 

Not budgeting for ongoing hosting and maintenance. Treating the initial build price as the total, one-time cost of having a website, without planning for the recurring costs covered earlier in this article, is one of the most common sources of budget surprise in the months after launch.

 

Skipping SEO foundations to save a small amount upfront. Basic on-page SEO — proper page titles, meta descriptions, a sensible site structure, reasonably fast load times — is far cheaper to build in from the start than to retrofit later, and skipping it to save a modest amount upfront often costs more in lost visibility over time than it saved initially.

 

Underestimating the value of good copywriting. A beautifully designed website with generic, unclear, or poorly written copy still struggles to actually convert visitors into customers. Copywriting is sometimes treated as an afterthought in budgeting, when it's often doing as much of the actual persuasive work as the design itself.

A Simple Website Budgeting Checklist

Before requesting quotes, it's worth working through this list:

  • List all required pages and functionality, as specifically as you can
  • Decide whether a template-based CMS approach or fully custom-coded build fits your needs and budget
  • Confirm whether you're providing content (copy, photos) or need it created, and budget accordingly
  • Set aside a separate line item for ongoing hosting, domain, and annual maintenance, not just the build
  • Get quotes from at least two or three vendors against the same written scope
  • Ask each vendor directly what's included, what counts as a change request, and what happens after handover

What This Looks Like for an Indian Small Business

To make this concrete: a Lucknow-based restaurant wanting a clean, mobile-friendly site with a menu, photos, location/hours, and a WhatsApp ordering link is a relatively contained, brochure-tier project — the kind of scope that a template-based CMS build with modest customization can handle well, at the lower end of the local market's typical range for a professionally built small business site. A growing D2C skincare brand wanting a full online store with a product catalog, payment gateway, inventory tracking, and order management is a meaningfully larger project, both because e-commerce functionality itself adds complexity and because a brand at that stage typically wants more custom design work to stand out — putting it at a noticeably higher point on the cost spectrum, though still well below what a fully bespoke enterprise platform would cost.

A third example is worth including because it shows how easily scope can shift mid-conversation: a growing real estate consultancy initially asked for "a simple 5-page website," but during scoping realized they also wanted a property listings section with filters, an inquiry form routed differently for buyers versus sellers, and integration with their existing CRM to avoid manually re-entering leads. None of these additions are unreasonable, but each one moves the project meaningfully up the complexity tiers described earlier — which is exactly why writing out your actual required functionality before requesting quotes, rather than assuming "simple website" covers whatever you eventually realize you need, leads to far more accurate, less frustrating quoting conversations.

Neither of these examples is a quote — they're illustrations of how the cost drivers covered throughout this article combine in practice. The right way to find your own number is still the same: define your scope clearly, and get it priced by more than one vendor.
